# Teach tables first, then serve pages

"Web part 2" is the right next course, but the order inside it matters. A standalone databases half should come first, followed by a dynamic-pages half that uses that database. Of the five candidates, databases fit the series best. SQL's filter-and-sort model continues the shell's grep, sort and head pipelines. It needs no loops for its core queries. It has the best no-install browser engine (real SQLite compiled to WebAssembly), and it ends naturally in a SQL-injection lesson that pays off the security course. Dynamic pages with htmx are a sound second half on one condition. Mainstream curricula reach server-rendered pages only through a programming language, but this course can drop that need by using logic-less, Mustache-style templates, a route table and a pretend server built into the page. Nobody has published a course for children built this way, so the design is new and should be tested with real learners. The research notes disagree on one point. Their topic ranking puts JavaScript ahead of dynamic web, because it assumes server pages need code, while the htmx research shows they do not. My recommendation is to keep "Web part 2" as databases followed by declarative dynamic pages, and to make a first programming language (JavaScript) the course after it. The evidence base is thin throughout. No study of under-16s learning SQL was found, and the evidence for SQL games comes mostly from adults. The design choices below therefore rest on curriculum precedent, research on university novices' errors, and engineering tests run by the researchers, not on trials with children.

## Children meet SQL through detective stories, with little proof it works

Curricula agree on a progression, and it is age-graded. At **9 to 10, England's Year 5 teaches flat-file databases without SQL**. Learners compare a paper database they built with a computer one, learn that "a database consists of 'records', and each record contains 'fields'", and choose which field to sort by to answer a question ([Raspberry Pi Foundation, Y5 L2](https://www.raspberrypi.org/curriculum/key-stage-2/data-and-information-flat-file-databases/computer-databases)). Mark Dorling's CS Unplugged / Digital Schoolhouse series for ages 6 to 16 goes further down the concrete end. Children *are* the records in three levels of "human databases" before any software appears. The series then reaches a plugged-in "Database Detectives" lesson and only after that "Relational Databases: Introduction" ([CS Unplugged classic](https://classic.csunplugged.org/activities/community-activities/databases/)). **At 13 to 14, schools write their own single-table SQL units**, because there is no official Teach Computing KS3 SQL unit. Nonsuch School's Year 9 unit, for example, covers SELECT, FROM, WHERE, AND/OR, LIKE with wildcards and the relational operators ([Nonsuch Y9](https://www.nonsuchschool.org/_site/data/files/quality-of-education/y9-curriculum/2C1B0E6F7DA6901F036721046E7F7ACB.pdf)). **Keys and joins arrive formally at GCSE (15 to 16)**. AQA's specification for first teaching from September 2025 requires table, record, field, data type, primary and foreign key, and the idea that relational design prevents inconsistency and reduces redundancy. It requires SELECT/FROM/WHERE/ORDER BY and INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE, and it caps exam queries at **"no more than two tables"** ([AQA 8525 §3.7](https://aqa.org.uk/subjects/computer-science/gcse/computer-science-8525/specification/subject-content/relational-databases-and-structured-query-language-sql)). Oak National Academy's nine-lesson GCSE unit runs flat file versus relational, then design, then SQL, and ends with data protection, privacy and the law. That is a ready link for a series that has just taught security ([Oak National Academy](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/curriculum/computing-secondary-aqa/units/databases-and-sql)).

**No curriculum for under-16s that this research found teaches indexes.** Even the GCSE ceiling stops at keys and two-table joins. Indexes, which the candidate course includes, are therefore enrichment material for this age. They are teachable only if the sandbox makes their effect visible (see the outline below).

Popular self-study resources split on one ordering question. **Khan Academy's Intro to SQL has learners CREATE a table and INSERT rows before they SELECT** ([Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=437)). The games instead hand over a finished database to investigate. In **SQL Murder Mystery** (Northwestern Knight Lab, by Joon Park and Cathy He), players query a SQLite database to find a killer in "SQL City". They check their answer by INSERTing a suspect into a `solution` table. The code is MIT-licensed and the text is CC BY-SA 4.0. It was inspired by Noah Veltman's command-line murder mystery set in "Terminal City", which gives a shell-literate audience a ready-made bridge ([NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries)). **SQL Island** packs about 30 levels into an hour-long escape adventure that needs no prior SQL ([SQL Island](https://sql-island.informatik.uni-kl.de/)). SQL Police Department casts the player as a police data analyst, adding ORDER BY, DISTINCT, GROUP BY and LIMIT in later cases ([Korben](https://korben.info/en/sql-police-department-learn-sql-solving-crimes.html)). The recurring metaphors are physical: record cards sorted by hand, children acting as rows, flat files framed by the trouble they cause, and databases contrasted with spreadsheets. The recurring story hook is crime. Common informal metaphors such as filing cabinets, library catalogues and book indexes turned up no sourced use with children.

The evidence that any of this works is weak, and the course should be honest about that. A review found **only three studies of games for teaching SQL and two of gamified practice systems**, and noted that the games are generally not available for replication ([MDPI Applied Sciences 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/13/6563)). The strongest result is a higher-education randomised trial with 30 students per arm, where an adaptive serious game beat a textbook ([UWS](https://research-portal.uws.ac.uk/en/publications/a-randomised-controlled-trial-to-evaluate-learning-effectiveness-/)). No learning-outcome study of SQL Murder Mystery, SQL Island or SQLPD with children was found. One cautionary result: with middle-schoolers, CS Unplugged activities only partly improved students' ideas of what computer science is, and their **intention to study it fell**. The activities were often not explicitly linked to the concepts they illustrated ([Taub et al.](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/cs-unplugged-and-middle-school-students-views-attitudes-and-inten/)). The story must serve the concept, not stand in for it.

The research on university novices' errors is large and points to where children will need help. It is not syntax. A study of 987 students found that **logical errors caused most failures**, while syntax and semantic slips were usually self-corrected. The most persistent errors were missing expressions, wrong or missing grouping columns, wrong comparison operators, **missing joins** and missing ordering. The authors recommend natural-language query planning ([Taipalus, Miedema and Aivaloglou, FIE 2020](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/73017)). An analysis of more than 160,000 query snapshots found semantic mistakes were mostly omissions, clustered in queries needing a JOIN, subquery or GROUP BY ([Ahadi et al., ITiCSE 2016](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/students-semantic-mistakes-in-writing-seven-different-types-of-sq/)). A think-aloud study names **"language-based" misconceptions**, where the English meaning of a keyword misleads, as one of four root causes ([Miedema et al., ICER 2021](https://icer2021.acm.org/details/icer-2021-papers/22/Identifying-SQL-Misconceptions-of-Novices-Findings-from-a-Think-Aloud-Study)). For 9-year-olds, whose English intuitions are strong and formal models weak, "show me the cats and dogs" written as `AND` is the obvious trap. This is an inference from the category, not a finding about children.

## No school course yet teaches pages made on the fly without code

Mainstream teaching of dynamic pages follows one order: request and response, then static versus dynamic, then templates, then forms with GET and POST, then cookies and sessions. MDN's learning area defines a template as the server "putting the data (from the Database) into placeholders inside an HTML template". It warns that URL parameters "can be changed by users and then resubmitted" and that GET requests must not update data. Its POST example already carries a CSRF token, and it teaches the redirect-after-POST pattern ([MDN Client-Server overview](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Extensions/Server-side/First_steps/Client-Server_overview)). These learners already know curl, GET and status codes, so they enter this sequence at the "template" rung. Every mainstream route then goes through code. CS50 reaches Flask routes, sessions and cookies only after eight weeks of C, Python and SQL ([CS50x Week 9](https://cs50.harvard.edu/x/weeks/9/)). Children's maker projects reach dynamic pages through Python Flask ([Random Nerd Tutorials](https://randomnerdtutorials.com/?p=18516)). **Courses for under-14s stop at static pages.** Code.org's Web Lab for grades 7 to 9 is HTML/CSS with JavaScript being added, and has no server side ([code.org/weblab](https://code.org/weblab)). Glitch, once the easy way to give students a small Node server, ended project hosting on 8 July 2025 ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/05/23/glitch_app_hosting_gone)). Child-level teaching offers metaphors rather than templates. A Year 6 role-play casts pupils as clients, waiters and kitchen ([Flip Education](https://flipeducation.ai/curriculum/gb/computing/year-6/the-world-wide-web-clients-and-servers)). A useful refinement separates "pre-made sandwiches (static files)" from food the chef cooks "fresh (dynamic content)" ([Codelivly via Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/854561674/Web-Server-Codelivly)). Sources disagree on whether the waiter stands for the network or the server ([Codefinity](https://codefinity.com/courses/v2/9c0efbb3-2ad2-40b7-ac7d-55708438f192/c48869fb-fd4d-4b1a-b305-7317edcd86ab/569619f7-7d5a-43f7-af28-ffc4740376db)). A course should drop the waiter entirely and keep the kitchen and the order slip.

**htmx is a good conceptual fit, but it has no record in schools.** Its documentation presents it as a generalisation of the anchor tag. Any element can make a request, on any event, with any HTTP method, and put the returned HTML into any target. The core attributes are `hx-get`/`hx-post`, `hx-trigger`, `hx-target` (a CSS selector) and `hx-swap` ([htmx docs](https://htmx.org/docs/)). The server returns HTML, not JSON, so all the logic stays in one place that children can inspect. The only confirmed academic course is Carson Gross's own undergraduate CSCI 431 "Hypermedia Systems" at Montana State ([Gross, MSU](https://www.cs.montana.edu/users/grug/)). The free *Hypermedia Systems* book is written for working developers and builds its example app in Flask ([hypermedia.systems](https://hypermedia.systems/book/contents/)). Other htmx teaching is commercial and aimed at adults. Flavio Copes's 45-lesson course lists HTML, HTTP and web forms as prerequisites ([Flavio Copes](https://flaviocopes.com/courses/htmx/)), and Accelebrate requires backend experience ([Accelebrate](https://www.accelebrate.com/training/htmx-web-apps)). No educator write-up, SIGCSE paper or talk about htmx for school students was found. This course would be first, with nothing to copy and nothing to warn it.

**The version situation is unsettled in late 2026.** htmx 4.0.0 shipped on 28 August 2026 under npm's `next` tag. It replaces XMLHttpRequest with `fetch()`, renames events (`htmx:afterRequest` becomes `htmx:after:request`) and some attributes (`hx-disable` becomes `hx-ignore`), and makes attribute inheritance explicit with an `:inherited` suffix. Forgetting that suffix can silently drop CSRF headers and produce 403s. The 2.x line is "supported indefinitely" ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/09/htmx-4-released/)). The npm `latest` tag is 2.0.11, published 22 September 2026, and htmx.org's main documentation still describes 2.x ([npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/htmx.org); [htmx docs](https://htmx.org/docs/)).

The key question is whether dynamic pages can be taught *before* programming. They can. **Mustache templates are explicitly "logic-less"**, with "no if statements, else clauses, or for loops". They need only three ideas: a named slot `{{name}}`, a section `{{#items}}…{{/items}}` that repeats once per item, and an inverted section `{{^items}}…{{/items}}` that shows when a list is empty. They also make the escaping decision visible: `{{x}}` is HTML-escaped and `{{{x}}}` is raw ([Mustache(5)](https://mustache.github.io/mustache.5.html)). Add a route table that maps a method and URL to data and a template, and the learner has a complete server-side model without writing a program. Hobbyists have already shown that htmx can be answered by a pretend server inside the browser. One htmx playground runs a Django-like mock server in a sandboxed iframe ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38906989)). Jake Lazaroff answered htmx from a service worker backed by IndexedDB ([jakelazaroff.com](https://jakelazaroff.com/words/building-a-single-page-app-with-htmx/)).

**The prerequisites** for this half are HTML structure and attributes, CSS id selectors (for `hx-target`), URLs including query strings, HTTP methods and status codes, and tables, rows and SELECT from the databases half. HTML forms are also needed and must be taught here if Web part 1 did not cover them. The sources name five **risks**:

1. **"It's all in my browser."** The best-documented misconception is that students "may not realize that websites need and use servers to store, process, and deliver content" ([CS Teaching Tips](https://www.csteachingtips.org/tip/misconception-students-may-not-realize-websites-need-and-use-servers-store-process-and-deliver)). A server simulated inside the page could reinforce exactly that belief.
2. **Action at a distance.** `hx-target` and `hx-swap` change a part of the page away from the element that triggered them. To a child this can feel like magic.
3. **htmx's own form of XSS.** Injected markup that carries `hx-` attributes can itself fire requests, so escaping has to cover attributes as well as scripts ([htmx docs, Security](https://htmx.org/docs/)).
4. **Version churn.** Learners who search online will meet both 2.x and 4.x syntax.
5. **Debugging pain with service workers.** Lazaroff called dev-tools support for service workers "absolutely abysmal" ([jakelazaroff.com](https://jakelazaroff.com/words/building-a-single-page-app-with-htmx/)).

## Databases fit best, but the field expects programming next

Every national progression checked puts **a text programming language directly after web development**:

| Progression | Sequence after web | Source |
|---|---|---|
| England, Teach Computing KS3 | Year 8 "Developing for the Web", then "Introduction to Python programming". Data science and cybersecurity come in Year 9. | [Teach Computing KS3](https://teachcomputing.org/curriculum/key-stage-3) |
| US, Code.org CS Discoveries | Web development, then JavaScript animations and games | [Code.org CSD one-pager](https://code.org/files/CSDiscoveries_1-pager.pdf) |
| Australia, Years 7 to 8 | A general-purpose language "like Python or JavaScript" | [Digital Technologies Hub](https://digitaltechnologieshub.edu.au/search/years-7-8-general-purpose-programming) |

None of these teaches relational SQL at 11 to 14. Networks come *before* web in Teach Computing, not after.

The 2026 growth area is AI and data. CSTA's 2026 middle-school standards add machine-learning and data-bias standards ([CSTA middle school](https://csteachers.org/middle-school-foundational-standards/)), and CYBER.ORG's version 2.0 standards add AI ([cyber.org/standards](https://cyber.org/standards)). Code.org is replacing CS Discoveries with AI Discoveries ([Code.org CSD](https://code.org/educate/csd)), and Experience AI is designed for exactly ages 11 to 14 ([Raspberry Pi](https://www.raspberrypi.org/?p=93154)). One counterweight matters for SQL-before-programming. No study was found that compares the two orders for children, but the design arguments favour data first. **Bootstrap:Data Science deliberately teaches tabular data without loops** so that weeks of loop practice do not delay data work ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/company/stories/bootstraps-data-science-course-for-middle-and-high-school-students)). SQL's English-like syntax is accessible "without requiring proficiency in other programming languages", although that research is on university students ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10480316-mining-sql-problem-solving-patterns-using-advanced-sequence-processing-algorithms)).

Ranked strictly by fit with what this series has already built, the order is as follows. The fit scores and reasoning are my synthesis of the cited facts above and in the next section.

| Rank | Candidate | What it reuses from the series | New load | Browser sandbox | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Databases (SQL)** | grep, sort and head become WHERE, ORDER BY and LIMIT. Permissions become who may change a table. Security leads into SQL injection. | Tables, types, keys, joins, aggregates. No loops. | Excellent: real SQLite in WebAssembly, about 440 KB gzip | Best next step |
| 2 | **Dynamic web with htmx (template-based, no code)** | HTML, URLs, HTTP, curl, publishing. SQL, if it follows databases. Escaping and CSRF from security. | Templates, routes, forms, GET versus POST, htmx attributes | Good, but needs a custom pretend server | Second half of Web part 2 |
| 3 | **Programming (JavaScript preferred)** | Shell scripts (sequence, variables), HTML pages to make interactive, git | Expressions, conditionals, loops, functions, debugging. The largest jump. | Excellent: runs natively in the browser | The course after Web part 2 |
| 4 | **AI and data** | Security (deepfakes, phishing, privacy). Tables, if after databases. | Training, testing, classification, bias | Good | Strongest 2026 momentum, but the weakest link to the shell, git and web arc. Run it as a parallel strand. |
| 5 | **Networking in depth** | Mostly already covered by URLs, DNS, HTTP and HTTPS | IP addresses, packets, routing, TCP/UDP | Poor: Filius is a desktop install and the browser simulators are unmaintained ([Linkletter](https://brianlinkletter.com/2023/03/network-simulators-for-high-school-teachers/)) | Last |

**This is where the notes disagree.** The topic-ranking research put JavaScript second and dynamic web fourth. Its reason was that dynamic web needs "a server-side language (variables, functions, conditionals, loops for rendering lists)" and so must come after programming. Its suggested path was SQL, then JavaScript, then htmx. That premise holds for every mainstream curriculum, but it does not hold for the design the htmx research found feasible. Mustache's slot, repeat and show-if-empty sections do the work of variables, loops and conditionals declaratively. A route table that pairs `GET /pets` with a SQL query and a template replaces the request handler. The new concepts in this half are therefore templates, routes and forms, each building on HTML and SQL the learner already has. That is a smaller step than a first language. It also completes the story the series started: the learner has published static pages on the pretend GitHub, and now sees why a static host cannot run a guestbook.

I recommend keeping dynamic web inside Web part 2, with programming as the next course. The pretend server's fixed rules then become the first thing learners rewrite as real code. The steelman for the other order is real: national progressions expect programming at this age, and the series is now behind them. If matching the national curricula matters more than narrative continuity, follow the ranking note's own fallback and teach programming before SQL. Even then, dynamic web should stay last.

## A Web part 2 outline in two halves: tables, then pages made to order

### The sandbox engine underneath

The researchers' engineering tests give clear choices. For SQL, use **sql.js 1.15.1's single-file inline build** (`sql-wasm-inline.js`, about 960 KB raw and 440 KB gzip). It needs no separate `.wasm` file, and its `exec()` result of `{columns, values}` maps directly onto a results table ([sql.js README](https://github.com/sql-js/sql.js); [npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/sql.js)). Load it lazily when the databases half opens.

The official `@sqlite.org/sqlite-wasm` 3.54.1 build is the alternative, at about 585 KB gzip with a separate `.wasm` fetch. Its one advantage is the `sqlite3_stmt_status` counter. In a test on 100,000 rows that counter read **99,999 rows stepped through without an index and 0 with one**, and the VM step count fell from 300,011 to 14 ([sqlite.org stmtstatus](https://sqlite.org/c3ref/c_stmtstatus_counter.html); [npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/@sqlite.org/sqlite-wasm)). Wall-clock time is a weak signal: 21 ms against 1 ms. For the single-table index lesson, sql.js can approximate the counter honestly. When the plan says `SCAN`, show the table's row count. When it says `SEARCH … USING INDEX`, show the matching rows. Both engines return those plan words from `EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN`. SQLite warns that the format "may change between SQLite releases", so pin the version and test the wording ([sqlite.org EQP](https://sqlite.org/eqp.html)). Avoid PGlite and DuckDB-WASM, which are 5 to 8 MB gzipped, and avoid AlaSQL, whose dialect is not real SQLite.

For the pretend web server, **rule out service workers**. They need a hosted worker file on an HTTPS or localhost origin, and sandboxed frames cannot register one ([MSW docs](https://mswjs.io/docs/integrations/browser); [Chromium review](https://codereview.chromium.org/2358993003)). Intercept in the page instead.

The two htmx versions offer different interception points, and here the notes disagree again. The dynamic-web research recommends the stable 2.x line. The sandbox research found that 4.0.0's per-request `ctx.fetch` hook, set in an `htmx:config:request` listener to return `new Response(html)`, is "far simpler and less fragile" than replacing `window.XMLHttpRequest` with a fake class for 2.0.11. Version 4.0.0 is also smaller: 13.0 KB gzip against 16.9 KB ([htmx 4.0.0 source](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/htmx.org@4.0.0/dist/htmx.js); [htmx 2.0.11 source](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/htmx.org@2.0.11/dist/htmx.js)).

My recommendation is to **pin htmx 4.0.0 for the engine, but teach only the attributes that behave the same in both versions**: `hx-get`, `hx-post`, `hx-target`, `hx-swap` (innerHTML, outerHTML, beforeend) and `hx-trigger`. Write every attribute directly on the element that uses it, so inheritance never comes up. Children never see a version-specific event name, and the course text can say "htmx" without a number. One caveat counts against this: htmx 4 still processes `<script>` elements in swapped content, and its equivalents of 2.x's `allowScriptTags` and `allowEval` switches were not confirmed. So run the learner's preview inside an `<iframe sandbox="allow-scripts">` without `allow-same-origin`. That frame has an opaque origin, so a successful XSS attack in it cannot reach the course page ([htmx docs, Security](https://htmx.org/docs/)). If the 4.x hook proves awkward in that frame, 2.0.11 with a fake XMLHttpRequest is the fallback. In either version, test the same-origin URL check against `about:srcdoc`, because the researchers did not verify it in a browser.

To counter the "it's all in my browser" misconception, make the server visible. The engine should record every request and response, showing the method, URL, `HX-*` headers, form body, status line and raw HTML. It should draw each exchange as a pair of letters, "to the server" and "back from the server", with the returned HTML shown as text next to the spot where it landed. The pretend shell's `curl` should hit the same pretend server and log the same letters.

### Part A — Tables (databases)

Each row below is one lesson: a short story, then "remember it" points, then a mission. The "introduces" column lists every new symbol or visual convention, so the author can check the series' rule that each concept is taught before it is used. The pre-built world should be a detective case that adapts SQL Murder Mystery's premise. Its CC BY-SA text licence requires attribution and share-alike for adapted text ([NUKnightLab](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries)).

| # | Concept | Introduces (symbols and conventions) | Needs | Browser mission |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Cards in a box: a table is records (rows) and fields (columns) | The grid picture, a header row, the words *table*, *row*, *column* | Files from Shell 1 | Drag paper "suspect cards" into piles to answer a question, then see the same cards as a table and sort by clicking a column header |
| A2 | Asking a table: `SELECT … FROM …;` | Capitalised keywords, the `;` that ends a question, `*` meaning "every column" | A1; shell `*` (contrast it explicitly) | List every name in `people`, then only names and ages |
| A3 | Picking rows: `WHERE` with `=`, `<`, `>`, `<>` | Single quotes around text, bare numbers, *text* versus *number* | A2; comparison from shell `test` if taught | Find the person named 'Rosa'. A lint message warns if double quotes are used. |
| A4 | Several rules: `AND`, `OR`, `NOT`, brackets | `( )` for grouping | A3 | Write the plan in words first ("people who are tall *and* have red hair"), then the SQL. One mission is built around the "cats and dogs" trap. |
| A5 | Ordering and top lists: `ORDER BY … ASC/DESC`, `LIMIT` | — | A2; Shell `sort` and `head` | Top five tallest suspects. A side panel shows the shell pipe equivalent. |
| A6 | Fuzzy search: `LIKE` | `%` (any letters) and `_` (one letter) | A3; grep | Partial number-plate clue. Shows that `LIKE` ignores case while `=` does not. |
| A7 | Empty and unknown: `NULL`, `IS NULL` | The "empty" chip in results | A3 | Find suspects with no alibi recorded |
| A8 | Counting and grouping: `COUNT`, `MIN`, `MAX`, `GROUP BY` | Function brackets `COUNT(*)` | A5 | How many gym visits did each member make? Plan which column to group by before writing. |
| A9 | Making your own table: `CREATE TABLE`, types, `INSERT INTO … VALUES` | Type names, `PRIMARY KEY` (id), comma-separated lists | A1 to A3 | Build a table of your own books. Use `STRICT` tables so wrong types cause errors. |
| A10 | Changing and removing: `UPDATE … SET … WHERE`, `DELETE FROM … WHERE` | — | A3, A9; permissions from Shell 2 | A missing `WHERE` updates every row. The "rebuild from the start" button teaches why backups matter. |
| A11 | Two tables that point at each other: why split, foreign keys | The arrow between tables in the schema picture, `REFERENCES` | A9 | Fix a single table where the owner's phone number is wrong in three places, then split it into `owners` and `pets` |
| A12 | Joining two tables: `JOIN … ON` | `table.column` dot notation | A11 | Link a car to its owner by id. The result checker warns when an `ON` is missing ("every pet paired with every owner"). |
| A13 (stretch) | Indexes: finding without looking at everything | `CREATE INDEX`, the plan words *SCAN* and *SEARCH* | A3; grep reads every line | A 100,000-row phone book. A torch animation sweeps every card while the counter shows "checked 100,000 rows". After `CREATE INDEX`, the pointer jumps and the counter shows "checked 1 row". |
| A14 | Case closed | — | All | Solve the full mystery, then check the answer by `INSERT` into `solution` |

Keep A1 to A8 as the core for younger learners. These lessons match what 13 to 14-year-olds do in Year 9 SQL units. A9 to A12 reach GCSE content and respect the two-table cap. A13 goes beyond every curriculum found. Following Khan's order (building before querying) would delay the story hook, so this outline queries a ready-made world first and builds tables second. Physical card-sorting (A1) keeps the concrete-first start that primary curricula use.

### Part B — Pages made to order (dynamic web)

| # | Concept | Introduces | Needs | Browser mission |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Cookies and sessions, the usual next rung ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Extensions/Server-side/First_steps/Client-Server_overview)), belong in a later course. Logins add state the learner cannot see, and they invite real-password habits.

The SQL-injection lesson works without the learner writing code. B9 shows two rules side by side: one rule pastes the order slip into the query text, the other fills a `?` blank. Using the `?` blank is the same idea as prepared statements in sql.js (`db.prepare` with `stmt.bind`). Because sql.js `exec` runs several statements, `'; DROP TABLE pets; --` really works on the learner's own copy, and a reset restores it. This mission design is my inference; the research found no children's course that demonstrates injection this way.

### Pitfalls the author must design around

The SQLite quirks are concrete and were verified in the researchers' tests:

1. **Double quotes do not cause an error.** `SELECT "nam" FROM pets` silently returns the text "nam" for every row ([sqlite.org quirks](https://sqlite.org/quirks.html)). SQL Murder Mystery's own solution example uses double quotes, so the habit is widespread ([NUKnightLab](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries)). Lint for it before running the query.
2. **`LIKE` ignores case but `=` does not.** Teach this explicitly, or turn it into a clue.
3. **Foreign keys are off by default.** Run `PRAGMA foreign_keys = ON`.
4. **Raw error messages are terse**, for example `near "SELEC": syntax error`. Translate them into kind sentences with a "did you mean" suggestion, and keep the original in small print.

The engineering risks are next:

1. **Runaway queries.** A recursive query or a huge join can freeze the tab. Run SQL in a Web Worker with a timeout.
2. **Unsafe rendering.** Render result cells with `textContent`, never `innerHTML`.
3. **Too many rows.** Cap the display at around 200 rows.
4. **Persistence.** Keep it minimal: a `db.export()` snapshot in localStorage behind a try/catch. Avoid OPFS, which only works in a Worker and, in its standard form, needs cross-origin isolation headers that a single page cannot set ([sqlite.org persistence](https://sqlite.org/wasm/doc/trunk/persistence.md)).

The teaching pitfalls follow from the "taught before used" rule:

1. **Three meanings of `*` and two wildcards.** In this series `*` already means "any filename" in the shell. SQL reuses it for "every column", and SQL's own wildcards are `%` and `_`. Children will mix these up unless each meaning is named when it appears.
2. **Checking the wrong thing.** The hard errors are omissions, such as a missing join condition, grouping column or sort ([Taipalus et al.](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/73017)). The result checker should compare result tables and point to what is missing, not just reject bad syntax.
3. **A story that hides the concept.** The detective framing must point at the idea, per the CS Unplugged warning.
4. **htmx feeling like magic.** Every swap should flash the changed element and appear in the letters panel.

## Conclusion

The series builds the same skill in each course: read what a system is doing by looking at its plain-text artefacts, such as files, commits, HTTP letters and permission bits. A database half followed by a template-and-route half keeps that skill unbroken. Every query, route and response stays visible as text the learner can read, and nothing yet needs the hidden state of a running program. That transparency is also the course's main risk. If the pretend server is too seamless, children will conclude that "the browser did it", so the letters panel and the shared curl view are essential, not decoration.

Two decisions are open, and only learners can settle them. The first is whether 9 to 11-year-olds can manage the GCSE-level half of Part A (keys and joins) or should stop at A8. The second is whether declarative templates really spare children the misconceptions that code-based servers produce; no research yet tests this. A small pilot of A1 to A8 and B1 to B6 with mixed ages would answer both before the stretch lessons are built. Programming then arrives with a ready-made purpose: rewriting the pretend server's fixed rules as real code.
