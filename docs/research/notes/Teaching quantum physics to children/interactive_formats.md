# Interactive, hands-on and game-based formats for teaching quantum physics to children (about 9 to 14)

Scope note: about 18 searches and fetches. Most primary papers were seen only as abstracts or search excerpts; where I read the full text (QuVis, Kohnle et al. 2015) it says so. Almost no evidence in this area comes from children aged 9 to 14. Every study below is flagged with the age of its participants.

## Simulations: QuVis, PhET, Quantum Composer, Quirk, IBM Quantum Composer

### Takeaway
The research-backed simulations (QuVis, PhET, Quantum Composer) were all built and evaluated with **university students**, using small samples and pre/post or think-aloud designs with no control group. Their strongest transferable lesson is design, not effect size: "implicit scaffolding" (constrained controls that guide without feeling guided), built-in challenges with a hint button, and linked representations. Quantum Composer's think-aloud study warns that a simulation can get students to the right answer without the reasoning.

### Cited Findings
- **QuVis (St Andrews, Kohnle et al.)**: "Enhancing student learning of two-level quantum systems with interactive simulations", *American Journal of Physics* 83(6), 560–566 (2015), DOI 10.1119/1.4913786. It reports that the simulations help learning at introductory and advanced **undergraduate** level, and that students perceive them as beneficial — [arXiv 1501.07905](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07905); [St Andrews repository](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/6149)
- QuVis evidence (full text read): in an advanced undergraduate course, 20 of 33 students completed the pre-test, the simulation homework and the post-test. On the built-in challenges, 80% identified the unknown mixture, 75% its fractions, 75% the superposition state and 70% its coefficients. The share of correct answers on an identical pre/post conceptual question (can a mixture be told apart from a superposition?) "substantially increased", and students were more confident. **No control group**, so this is weak evidence — [arXiv 1501.07905](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07905)
- QuVis design features (full text): the simulations "make the invisible visible", reduce complexity "to reduce cognitive load by eliminating extraneous material", link physical, mathematical and graphical representations (colour links outcome counts to probabilities), and use "implicit scaffolding through interaction design … guiding students without them feeling guided". Each simulation has built-in challenges with a **hint button**, and the activity has students explore known states before the unknown-state challenges. The simulations were refined iteratively through two-hour individual observation sessions — [arXiv 1501.07905](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07905)
- QuVis: a single-photon Mach-Zehnder simulation lets students insert filters that block half the energy, to test the misconception that superposition means a photon splits into two halves — [arXiv 1501.07905](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07905)
- QuVis: the paper also reports a comparison of a group using printed materials (N=34) with a simulation group (N=48) on a hidden-variable topic, and finds a marked difference in enjoyment ratings. I saw only the enjoyment result, not any learning comparison — [arXiv 1501.07905](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07905)
- QuVis has more than 90 simulations, and a subset is embedded in the Institute of Physics Quantum Physics resources — [search summary of QuVis materials](https://www.st-andrews.ac.uk/~crb6/research/papers/Kohnle_AJP2015_ESLTLQSIS.html) (secondary; not verified on the QuVis site)
- **PhET (Colorado)**: McKagan, Perkins, Dubson, Malley, Reid, LeMaster and Wieman, "Developing and Researching PhET simulations for Teaching Quantum Mechanics", *American Journal of Physics* (2008). It describes 18 quantum simulations built on visual models of the unobservable, interactive controls tied to animations, everyday connections and minimal calculation, all tested through student interviews and classroom trials (university level). I saw only the abstract, so I cannot report effect sizes — [arXiv 0709.4503](https://arxiv.com/abs/0709.4503); [PER-Central](https://www.per-central.org/items/detail.cfm?ID=10278)
- **Quantum Composer (Aarhus, Sherson group)**: Ahmed, Weidner, Jensen, Sherson and Lewandowski, *European Journal of Physics* 43, 065703 (2022). Think-aloud interviews with 5 second-year undergraduates. "Quantum Composer helps students to obtain the correct answers … but additional support is needed to facilitate the development of student reasoning behind these answers". Learning happened most when students visualised states, ran time dynamics and varied parameters repeatedly. Students misread the real and imaginary parts of the wavefunction — [ar5iv 2104.13260](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.13260); [JILA PDF](https://jila-pfc.colorado.edu/sites/default/files/2022-10/ejp_43_6_065703.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Right answer without the reasoning" (Quantum Composer) is the simulation version of "teaching the game, not the physics". A guided course should ask the child to **predict before running** and to **explain after**, not just to reach a target state.
- The QuVis hint button combined with built-in challenges is already close to the Jelly model (a step, a check, a "?"). That is a design precedent, not proof of effectiveness.
- The QuVis superposition-versus-mixture challenge ("is it 50/50 coins, or one thing in superposition? Measure along another axis to find out") is one of the few documented conceptual tasks that could be simplified for children.

### Gaps
- I found no evaluation of **Quirk** (Craig Gidney's browser circuit simulator) or **IBM Quantum Composer** as learning tools, and I did not fetch their sites. They are well-known circuit tools; any claim about their effectiveness for children would be unsupported.
- PhET's quantum learning-gain figures: I saw only the abstract.
- No simulation study found with participants under 16.

## Games: which have published learning-outcome studies, and what did they find?

### Takeaway
Most quantum games have **no published learning-outcome study**. The 23-author review of the field (Seskir et al. 2022) describes tools "by their developers" and reports no outcome data. The few studies are small and qualitative, mostly with ages 14 to 18. The one study in the target age range (Quander, ages 10 to 13) is focus groups only.

### Cited Findings
- **Review of the field**: Seskir, Migdał, Weidner, Wootton, Sherson, Chiofalo and others (23 authors), "Quantum games and interactive tools for quantum technologies outreach and education", arXiv 2202.07756 (2022), later in *Optical Engineering*. It covers Hello Quantum, Hello Qiskit, Particle in a Box, Psi and Delta, QPlayLearn, Virtual Lab by Quantum Flytrap, Quantum Odyssey, ScienceAtHome and the Virtual Quantum Optics Laboratory. The tools are "described by their developers" and **no evaluation or outcome data is reported** — [arXiv 2202.07756](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07756v1)
- **Hello Quantum (IBM / Basel, Wootton)**: a two-qubit puzzle game for non-experts and a gateway to IBM's platform; Wootton was scientific lead and IBM built the app to his specifications, iOS first. **I found no learning-outcome study** — [Seskir et al. workforce review, arXiv 2202.08940](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.08940); [University of Basel news (German)](https://www.unibas.ch/de/Aktuell/News/Uni-Research/Hallo-Quanten-Basler-Physiker-entwickelt-Spiele-App-mit-IBM.html)
- **Virtual Lab by Quantum Flytrap (Migdał et al.)**: a free, browser-based, no-code optical table. Drag-and-drop beam splitters, polarisers and detectors; real-time simulation of up to 3 entangled photons (paper) or 4 (current homepage; the sources conflict). It has a sandbox and a guided "Quantum Game" introduction, and open-source visualisation libraries (Quantum Tensors, BraKetVue). The homepage says it was used in quantum information courses at Oxford and Stanford (university level). **The paper is a description, not an outcome study** — [Optical Engineering 61(8) 081808, arXiv 2203.13300](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.13300); [Quantum Flytrap homepage](https://quantumflytrap.com/)
- **Quantum Tic-Tac-Toe (Goff)**: *American Journal of Physics* 2006. A classical game with one extra rule: each move places two "spooky" marks, and a measurement makes one real and erases the other. It was designed as a classroom metaphor for superposition, entanglement and collapse — [Goff, AJP (PDF copy)](https://agora.xtec.cat/iespuigcargol-calonge/wp-content/uploads/usu480/2019/01/QT3-AJP-10-20-06.pdf)
- **Quantum TiqTaqToe pilot**: *Education Sciences* 12, 446 (2022). 20 **high-school** volunteers had a short module on states, measurement, superposition and entanglement, then played a tournament. Analysis of two open-ended questionnaires: students grasped superposition and, with more difficulty, entanglement; the tournament format helped tie the game to learning goals. Small, qualitative, self-selected — [Education Sciences PDF](https://air.uniud.it/retrieve/3291f9d3-c88d-4431-98cc-1d9e90d3ac03/education-12-00446-v2.pdf); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/2f04afa5d3924d6284c2b8769df99732)
- **Quander (Chicago / Duke, SIGCSE TS 2026)**: two quantum mini-games teaching superposition, measurement and probability to **ages 10 to 13**. Results are focus-group vignettes showing "various levels of understanding". Qualitative, not pre/post — [SIGCSE 2026 paper page](https://sigcse2026.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2026-Papers/123/Quandray-Student-Conceptions-of-Quantum-Concepts-from-a-Gameworld)
- **Qupcakery (Chicago)**: a puzzle game (a virtual bakery) introducing gates, superposition and measurement, tested with middle and high schoolers. Reported findings are mostly descriptive (fun; some students linked superposition to the game's "mystery boxes") — [UChicago CS news](https://www.computerscience.uchicago.edu/news/virtual-bakery-game-serves-up-both-cupcakes-and-quantum-concepts-for-k-12-students/)
- **Qubit the Game** (card game): 16 to 17 year olds *self-reported* knowing more about quantum computing after playing — [K-12 framework review, arXiv 2512.18886](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.18886) (secondary summary)
- The same K-12 review notes "a lack of conclusive evidence that there is significant educational benefit for students participating in these simulations". For a physical "entanglement ball" game, no analysis has checked whether students picked up misconceptions — [arXiv 2512.18886](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.18886)
- **Quantum escape game** (grade 9 and up, 87 students): interest, perceived understanding and quality of collaboration were all rated significantly above neutral, with no differences by gender, grade or prior knowledge. These are **self-reports, not a learning test** — [AHFE conference paper](https://openaccess-api.cms-conferences.org/articles/download/979-8-950676-13-0_44)
- **Systematic review**: Yusuf, Román-González and Behera, "Quantum computing in K-12 education: an early systematic review", *Computer Science Education* (2025), DOI 10.1080/08993408.2025.2545915. I saw only the bibliographic record, not its findings — [UNED record](https://portalcientifico.uned.es/documentos/68b33d4e2f359d6e8f723e6d?lang=en)
- **Quantum Picturalism (Oxford / Quantinuum, Dündar-Coecke, Coecke et al.)**: diagrams (ZX-style) instead of matrices, complex numbers and trigonometry. 54 UK students **aged 16 to 18**, chosen at random from 734 volunteers, had 16 hours over 8 weeks. 82% passed an end-of-training exam built from university graduate-level questions, and 48% gained a distinction. **No control group reported** in the abstract, and the volunteers were self-selected — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01013v1); [arXiv 2512.00141](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.00141v1); [QCE 2023 origin paper, arXiv 2312.03653](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2312.03653)
- A trade-press piece says the most effective quantum education had narrative and collaborative elements. It is secondary, and I did not verify the study behind it — [The Quantum Insider](https://thequantuminsider.com/2022/07/06/how-effective-is-quantum-education-really/)

### Inferences
- For 9 to 14 year olds, no quantum game has evidence of learning. The best that can honestly be said is "children enjoyed it and some could explain superposition afterwards".
- Quantum Picturalism is the strongest evidence that a **visual formalism can stand in for the maths**, but its learners were 16 to 18, self-selected and highly motivated. Extending it to 9 to 14 year olds is a hypothesis.
- The TiqTaqToe pilot taught the concepts **before** the game. That supports "explain, then play", not "the game teaches by itself".

### Gaps
- **No learning-outcome studies found** for Quantum Odyssey, Quantum Chess, QPlayLearn, Particle in a Box, Qiskit Blocks, Entanglion, Q|Cards> or Hello Quantum. I did not fetch their websites, so I make no claims about their features.
- **Quantum Moves (ScienceAtHome)** is a citizen-science game. I found no learning study in this search, and I did not retrieve its research papers, which are about using player solutions in physics.
- Quander's full results and any pre/post data.

## Hands-on activities: polarisers, laser diffraction, single photons, Einstein-First, unplugged games

### Takeaway
Einstein-First (University of Western Australia) is the only programme found that has tested modern-physics teaching with children of the target age (Years 5 to 10). Its evidence is about **memorability and attitude, with small samples**, and I could not retrieve quantum- or photon-specific pre/post results. I found no outcome studies for the three-polariser demo, hair diffraction or unplugged card or dice games with children.

### Cited Findings
- Einstein-First Year 6 study (ages 10 to 11, 2011): six 45-minute lessons, a reading play and an excursion. Modern physics concepts "could be taught to upper-primary students", and the models and analogies were "highly memorable". A follow-up more than three years later re-contacted 11 of the 26 participants and found a lasting positive impression. The sample is small. The topic was mainly gravity and space-time, not quantum — [arXiv 2104.09773](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.09773); [UWA repository](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/long-term-impact-of-a-primary-school-intervention-on-aspects-of-e/)
- Einstein-First uses "toys, games, models and lasers". It has a Year 5 module "Fantastic Photons", and its trials follow a pattern of teacher training, pre-test, module and post-test, with about 1,600 student test records. It claims attitude gains, especially among middle-school girls. These are conference-level claims, not figures I could check — [OzGrav outreach page](https://outreach.ozgrav.org/portal2/programs/einstein-first/); [ICPE paper](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/ICPE/article/view/17012/14567); [MG15 abstract](https://icra.it/mg15/pdf/ED1-485KA920ER.pdf)
- Einstein-First's "Spin(ing) into the classroom" paper covers introductory quantum spin activities for **Years 6 to 10**, and reports that students enjoyed the content and found it relevant (attitude, not learning) — [Einstein-First published papers](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- The publications list includes a talk "Photon physics for high schools" (Blair, Kaur, Choudhary) — [MG15 programme](https://icra.it/mg15/FMPro%3F-db=3_main_mg15_.fp5&-format=detail_talk.htm&-lay=completo&-sortfield=lastname_and_name&attivo=Yes&-max=1500&-recid=33140&-token.0=19&-find=.html)

### Inferences
- The three-polariser demonstration maps directly onto a browser exercise (it is the same physics as QuVis's spin and photon simulations and Quantum Flytrap's polarisers). Its use for children rests on teaching practice, **not on outcome evidence I found**.
- Einstein-First's "memorable models and analogies" points to a strong concrete metaphor per idea as more important than formalism for 10 to 11 year olds. This is weak evidence.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Einstein-First's quantum or photon pre/post data.
- No evaluation found of laser-and-hair diffraction, single-photon demos, or QTEdu or Perimeter unplugged card and dice activities with children. I did not search Perimeter's resources directly.

## Design principles from the evidence

### Takeaway
General game-based learning evidence is moderate and positive (about g = 0.33), and it depends on design more than medium. Simpler visuals do as well or better. Worked examples with fading help near transfer. Hints help when they are read and hurt when they are clicked through; a "do it for me" button is effectively a bottom-out hint and needs guarding against abuse.

### Cited Findings
- **Games meta-analysis**: Clark, Tanner-Smith and Killingsworth, "Digital Games, Design, and Learning", *Review of Educational Research* 86(1), 79–122 (2016), K-16, studies 2000 to 2012. Games beat non-game instruction (effect 0.33, 95% CI 0.19–0.48, 57 comparisons). Enhanced game designs beat standard versions (0.34, CI 0.17–0.51, 20 comparisons). Effects varied with game mechanics, visual and narrative features and research quality: "the key role of design beyond medium" — [abstract via OpenDevEd](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/YHX5ZVR8); [SRI brief](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/digital-learning-pubs/digital-games-design-and-learning-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-brief/)
- In the same meta-analysis, games with simpler visual design did as well or better than visually complex ones (secondary report) — [Joan Ganz Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=16779)
- **Worked examples and fading** (Renkl, Atkinson and others): start with a fully worked example, then give problems where the learner completes more and more of the steps (backward fading removes the last step first). Fading improves near transfer and reduces errors compared with unfaded examples. The far-transfer evidence is weaker. One circuit-analysis study found forward fading with adaptive feedback did better than backward fading. Adaptive fading (keep fading on success, show the step again on failure) has been proposed — [Renkl et al. 2000, PsychArchives](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.11780/196); [ASU (Moreno) fading studies](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Stat_Adapt_Fad_JEE06.pdf); [ASU MoRO09](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/MoRO09.pdf)
- **Hint abuse**: in a Geometry Cognitive Tutor, students abused hints on 36% of actions (14% when rapid repeated requests count as one), mostly by clicking through to the bottom-out hint, which gives the answer. Time spent reading each hint was positively related to test performance, while how often hints were asked for was negatively related. In one study more hints went with more learning only in the high-level-hint condition — [LearnLab wiki: Help abuse](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse); [Cognitive Science Society paper](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/28865/galley/18736/download) (figures from secondary summaries; check against the Aleven et al. 2006 original)
- A WPI study asked whether it is better to give hints straight away or wait for the student to ask — [Razzaq & Heffernan 2010](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~nth/pubs_and_grants/papers/2010/ITS/Razzaq%20Hints%20Is%20It%20Better%20to%20Give%20or%20Wait.pdf) (I did not read its result)
- **Implicit scaffolding and linked representations** (QuVis; PhET design) — [arXiv 1501.07905](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07905); [arXiv 0709.4503](https://arxiv.com/abs/0709.4503)
- **Right answers without the reasoning** in an open simulation (Quantum Composer) — [ar5iv 2104.13260](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.13260)
- **A visual formalism can replace the maths** for motivated 16 to 18 year olds (Quantum Picturalism) — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01013v1)
- **Concept first, then game** (TiqTaqToe pilot) — [Education Sciences 2022](https://air.uniud.it/retrieve/3291f9d3-c88d-4431-98cc-1d9e90d3ac03/education-12-00446-v2.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Do it for me" is a bottom-out hint. The evidence says click-through hurts. Design judgement: after "do it for me", require a small prediction or a re-do of a near-identical step before the reward, so the reward isn't earned by the button alone.
- Fading maps onto the steps of an exercise: the first exercise of each idea is worked, and later ones remove steps. Keep it adaptive (show the step again on failure).
- A "playable language" (circuit or arrow diagrams) is supported only for older learners. For 9 to 14 year olds, one or two visual conventions (an arrow or dial for a state, a box for a gate) is design judgement.
- **Teaching the game, not the physics**: three countermeasures have some support. Teach the idea before the game (TiqTaqToe). Ask for predictions and explanations (Quantum Composer). Keep visuals simple (Clark et al.).

### Gaps
- None of the hint, fading or games-meta evidence is about quantum physics or ages 9 to 14 specifically. It is transferred from maths, geometry and engineering.
- I did not retrieve Wouters et al. (2013) or other game meta-analyses.

## A proposed first browser module (10 to 15 exercises, one idea each)

### Takeaway
The best-supported spine is the **polariser or spin "measurement" story** (QuVis, Quantum Flytrap, the three-polariser demo, Einstein-First's photons). It needs only angles and counting, no complex numbers. Each exercise follows predict, run, explain, with hints and a guarded "do it for me". Most of the sequence is **design judgement**; labels say which parts lean on evidence.

### Cited Findings
- The evidence anchors for the sequence are listed in the sections above: QuVis (photon and spin measurement, superposition versus mixture, hint and challenge), Quantum Flytrap (browser polarisers, beam splitters, detectors), TiqTaqToe (concept before game), Quantum Composer (predict and explain), Clark et al. (simple visuals), fading and hint-abuse research.

### Inferences (proposed sequence; E = leans on cited evidence, D = design judgement)
1. **Light gets through, or it doesn't.** One polariser and a lamp; turn the filter and watch the brightness. (D; three-polariser practice, no outcome study found)
2. **Two filters at right angles block everything.** Predict, then try. (D)
3. **Light comes in lumps.** Turn the lamp down until single "clicks" arrive at a detector. One photon is one click, never half a click. (E: QuVis tests the "photon splits in half" misconception directly)
4. **At 45°, each photon is a coin toss.** Send 10, then 100 photons and count the clicks. Probability comes from counting. (E: QuVis links counts to probabilities; Quander targets measurement and probability for ages 10 to 13, qualitative only)
5. **The angle sets the odds.** Try 0°, 30°, 45°, 60° and 90°, and draw the click fraction. (D)
6. **Measuring changes the photon.** After passing a 45° filter, the photon "is" 45°. (D)
7. **The three-filter surprise.** Put a 45° filter between two crossed filters, and light comes back. Ask for a prediction first. (E for predict-first: Quantum Composer's "correct answers without reasoning"; the demo itself is D)
8. **A worked example, faded.** The same three-filter question at new angles: first fully worked, then with one step to fill in, then solo. (E: worked examples and fading, near transfer, from non-quantum domains)
9. **Superposition versus "we just don't know".** Two mystery boxes of photons: one a 50/50 mix of horizontal and vertical, one all at 45°. The same 0° filter gives the same counts; find the filter that tells them apart. (E: QuVis's superposition-versus-mixture challenge, simplified; evidence from undergraduates)
10. **One picture for a state.** Introduce an arrow (or dial) as the state, and a box as "something that turns the arrow". Only one convention. (E, weak: Quantum Picturalism, ages 16 to 18; simplicity: Clark et al.)
11. **Beam splitter: one photon, two paths.** One photon reaches exactly one detector, 50/50. (D; Quantum Flytrap's browser model)
12. **Interference: two paths can cancel.** A Mach-Zehnder arrangement where one detector never clicks. Block one path and it starts clicking. (E: QuVis Mach-Zehnder simulation, undergraduates)
13. **Play the idea: Quantum Tic-Tac-Toe** (a short version), now that superposition and measurement are taught. (E: concept-before-game, TiqTaqToe pilot, high school)
14. **Explain it to a friend.** A choice-based explanation check, free of jargon, that rewards the reasoning, not the result. (E: hints, reasoning, Quantum Composer)

Guardrails (design judgement, grounded in the hint-abuse research): "do it for me" counts as the bottom-out hint and is followed by a quick near-identical re-do before the reward. Hints escalate from a question, to a pointer, to the answer. Visuals stay simple. Entanglement is left out of the first module, because even 16 to 18 year olds found it hard in the TiqTaqToe pilot. Every term (photon, filter, probability, superposition, state) needs a plain-English definition before use, as the Jelly teaching rules require.

### Gaps
- No study supports this specific sequence, or polariser-first teaching, for ages 9 to 14. It should be tested with children (observation sessions in the QuVis manner are a cheap, evidenced way to refine it).
- Whether 9 to 11 year olds can handle "fraction of clicks" (proportional reasoning) is untested here. It may push the lower age bound to about 11.
- How well the polariser model fits the real behaviour of light at each step, and where the sandbox must say it differs from a real experiment, needs checking by a physicist.
