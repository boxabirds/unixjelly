# Quantum education programmes for children aged about 9 to 14: what each does and the evidence behind it

Scope note: these notes were gathered on 28 October 2026 with a limited number of searches (about 20 calls). The only programmes studied in detail are Quantum Picturalism, Einstein-First and QuanTime. Several of the programmes the brief named have no published evaluation that I could find, and the Gaps sections say which ones. Overall, almost no evidence from controlled trials exists for the 9 to 14 age group. Most measured outcomes come from older pupils (15 to 18), or are attitude and enjoyment self-reports with no control group.

## Quantum Picturalism / ZX-calculus (Coecke, Quantinuum, "Quantum in Pictures")

### Takeaway
The 2023 UK experiment is real, but it was an uncontrolled pilot with a single post-test. The pupils were aged 16 to 18 (mean 16.84), not 15 to 17, and none were in the 9 to 14 band. It ran online with volunteers, and the main authors marked the exam. It did not compare the pupils with Oxford postgraduates directly. The pupils sat an exam "usually given to" Oxford postgraduates, so "beat Oxford postgraduates" is a press framing that the paper's own text does not support. The evidence is weak to moderate for "teenagers can learn the diagrammatic formalism" and absent for under-15s.

### Cited Findings
- **Design.** Exploratory, single post-test design with no control group. Pupils were recruited from volunteers by purposive sampling. Sample size was limited by the number of tutors available. — [arXiv 2504.01013, Dündar-Coecke et al., "Making the quantum world accessible to young learners through Quantum Picturalism: An experimental study"](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)
- **Sample.** 75 volunteers were recruited and 54 were analysed. The paper does not explain the 21 who are missing. Ages were 16 to 18: 59.6% were 17, about 25% were 16 and 9.6% were 18 (mean 16.84, SD 0.58). Pupils came from 37 schools in 24 UK cities: 30 state, 14 grammar, 3 state boarding, 2 private boarding, 2 private independent and 3 missing. — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)
- **Selection, as described in the later paper.** The 54 were "randomly selected from a pool of 734 volunteers across the UK". This differs from "purposive sampling" in the 2025 paper, and a self-selected pool of volunteers is still not a representative sample. — [arXiv 2512.00141, Coecke, Kissinger, Gogioso, Dündar-Coecke, Khan et al., "High schoolers excel at Oxford quantum course using pictorial mathematics" (Nov 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.00141)
- **Format.** Weekly one-hour live online tutorials from 5 June to 11 August 2023: eight weeks with a one-week break, then three weeks for the post-training exam. The abstract says "16 hours of training", which does not match eight one-hour sessions, and the paper does not reconcile the two. — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)
- **The exam.** The pass mark was 50%, and two "canary" questions had to be passed. Bob Coecke and Aleks Kissinger, who are authors and the developers of the formalism, marked double-blind. Stefano Gogioso marked 10 scripts as a third marker where the first two differed by more than 9 points. Coecke's marks were systematically higher than Kissinger's (t(53) = 4.57, p < .001). — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)
- **Results.** Mean 57.93 (SD 26.90). Raw marks gave 20 distinctions, 7 merits, 9 passes, 3 near-fails and 15 fails. With the canary criteria applied, the reported figures are "82% passed, 48% distinction". — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1); headline repeated in [arXiv 2512.00141](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.00141)
- **The Oxford comparison.** The paper describes the exam as one "usually given to Oxford University postgraduates". The text I read compares no scores with Oxford students. — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)
- **Attitude survey, post-course only.** 67.8% agreed or strongly agreed that the course strengthened their motivation or confidence (28.8% not sure). 86.4% agreed or strongly agreed that they were motivated towards a STEM career. 76.3% enjoyed it or extremely enjoyed it. — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)
- **Limitations the authors state.** They call it a proof of concept and say more diverse age, ability and cultural groups are needed. Long-term retention was not measured. Several findings are correlational. — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)
- **Affiliations and conflict of interest.** Most authors are at Quantinuum (Oxford). The others are at Oxford Computer Science and Physics, UCL Centre for Educational Neuroscience, City University London, IBM Research, Moth Quantum, Hashberg Ltd and the Perimeter Institute. Ilyas Khan, Quantinuum's founder, is an author of the 2025 paper. The text I read did not show the competing-interests section. — [arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1); [arXiv 2512.00141](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.00141)
- **Publication history.** An earlier conference paper, "Quantum Picturalism: Learning Quantum Theory in High School", appeared at IEEE QCE 2023. A version was presented at the Applied Category Theory 2024 conference in Oxford. A paper titled "Beyond symbolic algebra with quantum picturalism" appears on PubMed Central, which suggests journal publication, but I did not open it to confirm the venue or contents. — [search summary citing IEEE QCE 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01013); [ACT 2024 paper PDF](https://oxford24.github.io/assets/act-papers/49_high_schoolers_excel_at_oxford.pdf); [PMC13341600](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13341600/)
- **No follow-up trial found.** The November 2025 arXiv paper reports the same 2023 cohort and the same headline figures, and calls itself a "pilot study". — [arXiv 2512.00141](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.00141)

### Inferences
- Markers who invented the formalism, with an employer that sells products built on it, are a real conflict of interest. Double-blind marking between those two authors reduces the risk but does not remove it. The 21 participants who went missing (75 recruited, 54 analysed) could raise the pass rate if those who dropped out were weaker.
- The canary rule turns a mean of 57.9%, with a large spread and 15 raw fails, into "82% pass". The headline depends on how the paper defines a pass.
- These were motivated 16 to 18-year-olds volunteering for a summer course. The results say nothing directly about 9 to 14-year-olds. Some caution would apply even to typical 17-year-olds.
- What the study supports: diagrams can let some teenagers without advanced maths do non-trivial quantum circuit reasoning. It does not show that pictures work better than the standard approach, because no comparison group was taught another way.

### Gaps
- I could not read the conflict-of-interest and funding statements: the fetch was truncated at 100,000 characters.
- I did not confirm whether 2504.01013 or 2512.00141 has passed peer review in a journal. The PMC entry needs checking.
- I found no 2024 to 2026 trial with younger pupils or a control group, despite search. Some may exist that I missed.
- I did not trace where "beat Oxford postgraduates" first appeared in press coverage.

## Einstein-First (University of Western Australia)

### Takeaway
Einstein-First is the programme with the most published research that actually covers ages 7 to 15, including quantum topics: photons, phasors, spin and quantum probability. Almost all of it comes from the project team. Samples are small to moderate, designs are mostly pre/post without control groups, and much of it sits in practitioner journals (Physics Education) or is unpublished. The finding repeated across studies is that girls start with lower interest or attitude scores and roughly catch up with boys by the end. The evidence is moderate for "feasible and engaging at ages 10 to 15" and weak for causal learning gains.

### Cited Findings
- **14 to 15-year-olds, 2013 and 2014 cohorts combined.** 102 students (57 male, 45 female). Males began more interested in physics. Females' interest rose significantly after the programme. — [arXiv 1712.02063, "Evaluation of 14 to 15 Year Old Students' Understanding and Attitude towards Learning Einsteinian Physics"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02063)
- **Gender response across interventions.** Female knowledge gains were slightly larger than males' after each intervention. Before one long programme, 88% of males and 62% of females agreed that "physics is interesting". In another cohort, pre-programme interest was as low as 22% of males and 8% of females, so the pattern varies by cohort. Significantly more females than males disliked calculations. — [arXiv 1712.06323, "Gender response to Einsteinian physics interventions in school"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06323)
- **Year 7.** 120 students, 20 lessons. Near-universal understanding of core concepts, and girls' interest increased significantly. Published in Research in Science Education in 2019. — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- **Years 6 to 10.** Girls started with lower attitude scores and matched boys by the end (Kaur et al. 2020). — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- **One-day programme, Years 7 to 10, on gravitational waves and quantum physics.** Prior knowledge and age had no significant effect on learning core concepts. Short programmes were not enough for derived concepts (Choudhary et al., Physics Education, 2018). Longer programmes improved derived concepts, with differences by academic talent and gender (Choudhary et al. 2021). — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/); [ResearchGate: short vs long interventions](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342665173_A_Comparison_of_Short_and_Long_Einsteinian_Physics_Intervention_Programmes_in_Middle_School)
- **Year 6 enrichment, one class.** 26 students. Statistically significant gains in conceptual understanding, varying by concept (Pitts et al., Research in Science Education, 2014). — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- **Long-term follow-up.** Pupils taught at ages 10 to 11 in 2011 were followed up in 2020. Of the 11 who took part, recall of concepts such as curved space-time lasted (Adams et al., Physics Education, 2021). — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- **Quantum activities for younger pupils.** Phasor wheels for quantum electrodynamics, Year 9 and middle school (Choudhary and Blair, Eur. J. Phys., 2021): no sample size given. "Spin(ing) into the classroom", Years 6 to 10, 2024: students enjoyed it and found it relevant, with no quantitative results. Quantum probability and spin with physical models, ages 11 to 15, 2025: "successfully trialled", with no numbers reported. — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- **Primary maths strand, ages 7 to 14.** About 700 participants over nearly 200 hours, and in a later paper 300+ teaching hours. Reports enthusiasm, knowledge gains and more positive attitudes to maths, but the list gives no effect sizes. — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- **The project's own summary claim.** "Curriculum trials consistently show positive outcomes, including gender equalisation" is promotional wording from the project's site. — [Einstein-First published papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/)
- **Book chapter overview.** Maxwell, Blair, Kaur, Choudhary and Zadnik, "Einstein-First", Routledge. — [Taylor & Francis chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003161721-26/einstein-first-carolyn-maxwell-david-blair-jyoti-kaur-rahul-choudhary-marjan-zadnik)

### Inferences
- The gender-equalisation finding replicates across several cohorts from the same team. That makes it the most promising signal in this whole area for 10 to 14-year-olds. It still comes without control groups, so regression to the mean and novelty effects can't be ruled out.
- The finding that short programmes don't reach derived concepts matters for design. One-off sessions can teach the headline ideas, but deeper ideas need longer exposure.
- Einstein-First teaches quantum with physical models (toy projectiles for photons, phasor wheels, spinning tops). Concrete analogies are its approach, rather than the formalism Quantum Picturalism uses.

### Gaps
- No independent, non-UWA evaluation was found.
- Effect sizes and statistical details for the Year 7 (n = 120) and Years 6 to 10 gender studies were not extracted. Only the project's summary list was read.
- Most papers from 2024 to 2026 report no outcome data.

## Quantum computing-first programmes (IBM/Qiskit, Microsoft Quantum Katas, Q-CTRL Black Opal, Quantum Quest, Qubit by Qubit, Quantum for All, QuanTime, Perimeter Institute)

### Takeaway
Reach is large, but there is no rigorous evaluation of learning outcomes for any of these at ages 9 to 14 that I could find. Qubit by Qubit reports scale and confidence data only in conference abstracts, with no numbers. Black Opal's outcome figures are company case studies of university and adult learners. QuanTime, which is the most relevant to the target age, has only a teacher survey with a low response rate and no measured student outcomes. Quantum Quest was found only as a programme description.

### Cited Findings
- **Qubit by Qubit (The Coding School): history.** Started in 2020 with MIT, running a one-week summer camp taught by MIT researchers and a year-long course with IBM Quantum. — [MIT News, Aug 2020](https://news.mit.edu/2020/mit-researchers-lead-high-school-quantum-computing-educational-initiative-0810)
- **Qubit by Qubit: 2021 APS abstract.** Reported on a one-week summer programme of about 300 students and an eight-month course of about 8,000 students, over 50% from groups under-represented in STEM. It analysed prior knowledge and attitudes but gave no pre/post figures. — [APS March Meeting 2021 abstract](https://archive.aps.org/mar/2021/a29/9)
- **Qubit by Qubit: 2023 APS abstract.** Of about 7,000 high-school-age students surveyed from over 2,000 schools, over 80% had never had a formal lesson on quantum. It lists a Middle School Summer Camp and promises data on STEM self-confidence by age, without numbers. — [APS March Meeting 2023 abstract](https://archive.aps.org/mar/2023/k34/11); [ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023APS..MARK34011P/abstract)
- **Qubit by Qubit: a related case study.** "Teaching Quantum Computing to High-School-Aged Youth: A Hands-On Approach" describes the programme, including outreach to middle schools. I could not confirm whether it reports outcome data. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356206341_Teaching_Quantum_Computing_to_High-School-Aged_Youth_A_Hands-On_Approach)
- **Qubit Touchdown board game, not Qubit by Qubit.** 107 public high-school students in seven classes on one school day, with a single post-activity survey. Interest rose slightly and self-confidence rose more, and 83.2% would recommend the game as a learning tool. — [arXiv 2501.10449](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10449)
- **Q-CTRL Black Opal.** Company case study at the University of Hull: "85% of students reported that Black Opal improved their overall learning outcomes" (self-report, undergraduates). — [Q-CTRL case study](https://q-ctrl.com/case-study/improving-quantum-education-outcomes-with-black-opal-at-the-university-of-hull-uk). An independent sprint report from SC Quantum (Nov 2025) praised its interactivity and pacing but noted that some exercises "felt forced". — [SC Quantum SCQ25 EDU Sprint Report](https://www.scquantum.org/sites/default/files/SCQ25-EDU-Sprint-Report-NOV26.pdf)
- **Quantum Quest (QuSoft, Amsterdam).** A bilingual online course teaching quantum programming to high-school students. One 2020 round had 100+ Dutch students, 15 teachers and 20 international students, and the description gives no outcome data. The University of Amsterdam publications repository has a record titled "The Quantum Quest" that I did not open. — [QuSoft news, Nov 2020](https://qusoft.org/2020/11/17/students-learn-how-to-program-for-the-quantum-computer/); [UvA record](https://handle.uba.uva.nl/personal/pure/en/publications/the-quantum-quest(33ee01f5-732f-4b58-b16d-23db6008eb64).html)
- **QuanTime: what it is.** Pre-packaged activities modelled on "Hour of Code", created and run by the US National Q-12 Education Partnership. Topics are wave-particle duality, electron transitions, entanglement, art and polarisation, and quantum games. The aim is no extra preparation for teachers. — [Ghimire, Kashyap, Edwards, Franklin, Singh, arXiv 2510.15785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15785)
- **QuanTime: evaluation.** A teacher survey (12 Likert items, 14 open-ended questions). 132 teachers were invited from the Q-12 mailing list and 36 completed it. Of the 35 analysed, 12 taught grades 4 to 8 (about ages 9 to 14) and 23 taught grades 9 to 12. About two-thirds had used QuanTime. Teachers of grades 4 to 8 favoured Art and Polarisation, and teachers reported that students enjoyed the activities. The authors state that the study "did not assess student understanding or learning outcomes". They also flag the risk of non-response bias. — [arXiv 2510.15785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15785). An author is at the University of Chicago and another at Duke, and Emily Edwards is associated with the Q-12 programme. That is an insider evaluation, although this is my inference from the affiliations.
- **Quantum for All.** Teachers in the QuanTime survey mentioned it as a resource, but I found no evaluation of it in this search. — [arXiv 2510.15785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15785)
- **US Q-12 landscape.** A survey of US programmes (Oct 2025) found K-12 quantum education "unevenly distributed and insufficiently documented". Effective programmes emphasise hands-on learning, teacher professional development, early career awareness and alignment with standards. — [arXiv 2512.18886](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2512.18886)

### Inferences
- In the computing-first space, evaluation consists mostly of reach numbers ("10,000 students"), satisfaction and confidence self-reports. No learning-outcome studies with comparison groups were found for any age, and none at all for 9 to 14.
- Company-run figures (Q-CTRL, and to a lesser degree Quantinuum's Quantum Picturalism and IBM-partnered courses) should be reported as claims, not as measured effects.

### Gaps
- **IBM Quantum / Qiskit educational material, Microsoft Quantum Katas, Perimeter Institute resources, "Quantum Camp", QubitxQubit beyond what is above.** I found no published evaluations. I did not search each one separately because of the limit on tool calls, so this is "not found", not "does not exist".
- **The Qubit by Qubit middle-school camp.** No published outcome data was found.
- **Quantum Quest.** The brief asked about "Ivo Pots"; I could not verify any connection to Quantum Quest or find an evaluation.

## European Quantum Flagship QTEdu pilots and the Competence Framework

### Takeaway
QTEdu ran 11 pilots, two of them at school level (PHONQEE and PCK). The publications I found describe designs and instruments, not measured outcomes for pupils. The Utrecht pilot is building a Quantum Concept Inventory, which shows that a validated test for secondary pupils did not yet exist.

### Cited Findings
- QTEdu, the Quantum Flagship education working group, launched 11 pilots across schools, higher education, the workforce and the public. — [qt.eu news](https://qt.eu/news/2022/11-pilot-programs-education)
- As of 2021 there were two high-school pilots. PHONQEE uses games and hands-on experiments. PCK maps core quantum concepts to teaching strategies. — [arXiv 2303.07055, "Contributions from Pilot Projects in Quantum Technology Education as Support Action to Quantum Flagship"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.07055)
- The Utrecht-led pilot aims to build a Quantum Concept Inventory for secondary-level understanding of quantum concepts, starting with a Delphi study of experts. — [Utrecht research portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/community-based-development-of-the-quantum-concept-inventory-in-c/)
- Four QTEdu pilots are written up in *Challenges in Physics Education* (Springer, 2024), pp. 219 to 238. I did not read the full text. — [arXiv 2303.07055 / TU Delft record](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/contributions-from-pilot-projects-in-quantum-technology-education/)
- A GIREP 2022 session noted that "a thorough evaluation with reliable and validated instruments is still missing" for two-state approaches to teaching quantum physics. — [CERN Indico, GIREP 2022 session](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1092708/sessions/443681/)

### Inferences
- In Europe, effort has gone into frameworks and instruments, not into trials at school level. The 9 to 14 band is barely addressed.

### Gaps
- **The Quantum Technology Competence Framework.** Not researched in this session, so I have no sources on whether it covers school level.
- I found no pilot outcome data for pupils from PHONQEE or PCK.

## National curricula and pilots that teach quantum earlier

### Takeaway
I found evidence of research-led pilots at lower secondary level (around age 13 to 15) in Germany ("Spin First") and the Netherlands (TU Eindhoven, aiming at awareness), and an international study of two-state systems (Italy, Hungary, Slovenia and Germany). In the US, the Q-12 Partnership supports activities rather than a curriculum, and the US standards (NGSS) prescribe no quantum content. In Australia, Einstein-First is a curriculum proposal, not national policy.

### Cited Findings
- **Germany.** A "Spin First" teaching approach introduces quantum physics qualitatively at lower secondary level through two-state systems, with first empirical findings (EPJ Quantum Technology, 2025). — [EPJ QT 2025 article](https://epjqt.epj.org/articles/epjqt/abs/2025/01/40507_2025_Article_436/40507_2025_Article_436.html)
- **Several countries.** A team from Italy, Hungary, Slovenia and Germany published first empirical findings on learning quantum physics through two-state systems in Physical Review PER. — [idw news](https://idw-online.de/de/news843754); [PRPER article](https://journals.aps.org/prper/pdf/10.1103/d1yx-kfsz)
- **Netherlands.** A TU Eindhoven project adds experiments to existing lower-secondary topics to create "quantum awareness". — [TU/e research portal](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/creating-a-quantum-awareness-in-lower-secondary-science-education/). Dutch upper secondary schools also have an optional NLT module on quantum computing ("Ongehoorde kansen met Quantum"). — [Woudschoten 2024 workshop report](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/woudschotennatuurkunde/verslagen/Vrsl2024/_downloads/6c2f61c7bf2b0798c46b7a0ed13248a3/Template%20WND%20verslag%20wg%2025%20-%20Michiel%20Thijssen.docx)
- **United States.** NGSS prescribes no quantum content beyond atoms and the periodic table. QuanTime is run by the National Q-12 Education Partnership as a voluntary activity. — [arXiv 2510.15785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15785)
- **Expert consensus.** A 2025 Delphi study looked at experts' views on the future of quantum physics teaching at secondary level. — [EPJ QT 2024 article](https://epjqt.epj.org/articles/epjqt/abs/2024/01/40507_2024_Article_237/40507_2024_Article_237.html)
- **A "global perspective" paper.** Gragera Garcés et al., May 2025, argue that adding quantum computing to high-school curricula is "feasible" and propose a modular strategy. The abstract reports no pilot outcomes. — [arXiv 2505.14809](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14809)

### Inferences
- I found no national curriculum that teaches quantum to ages 9 to 14 as statutory content. Where quantum appears, it is in upper secondary (about ages 16 to 18) or in research pilots.

### Gaps
- I did not extract the ages, sample sizes or effect sizes of the German "Spin First" study or the international PRPER study.
- I did not verify the Italian national curriculum position, Australian state curricula, or England.

## Systematic reviews and meta-analyses, 2023 to 2026

### Takeaway
Reviews exist, but none I could read targets ages 9 to 14, and I found no meta-analysis. The closest is "Quantum computing in K-12 education: an early systematic review" (Computer Science Education, 2025/2026). Its title calls it "early", which signals that the evidence base is small.

### Cited Findings
- Yusuf, Román-González and Behera, "Quantum computing in K-12 education: an early systematic review", *Computer Science Education* 36(3), 461 to 493, DOI 10.1080/08993408.2025.2545915. I could not get the abstract. — [UNED portal record](https://portalcientifico.uned.es/documentos/68b33d4e2f359d6e8f723e6d?lang=en); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394737109_Quantum_computing_in_K-12_education_an_early_systematic_review)
- "Empirical insights into the effects of research-based teaching strategies in quantum education", *Phys. Rev. PER* 20, 020601 (2024). A systematic review of quantum education research from 2017 onwards covering school and university. My search tool attributed it to "Marshman et al.", but I did not verify the authors, and that attribution may be wrong. — [PRPER](https://link.aps.org/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.20.020601); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386423496_Empirical_insights_into_the_effects_of_research-based_teaching_strategies_in_quantum_education)
- "Teaching Quantum Physics at Secondary Schools: A Systematic Literature Review" (uploaded 2024) reports 263 suitable articles. The venue is unconfirmed. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375900038_Teaching_Quantum_Physics_at_Secondary_Schools_A_Systematic_Literature_Review)
- Song, Wang and Ghannam, a review of XR in quantum technology education (*Computers & Education: X Reality*, Dec 2024). Studies focused on knowledge acquisition more than skills or engagement. Not specific to schools. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949678024000370)
- A June 2026 resource letter on research in quantum information education (arXiv 2606.06445) exists. I did not read it. — [arXiv 2606.06445](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.06445)
- Darienzo and Kelly (IEEE QCE 2024) reviewed quantum information programmes for high-school students. I saw it only cited in another paper. — [search summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394737109_Quantum_computing_in_K-12_education_an_early_systematic_review)

### Inferences
- Across what I read, the same weaknesses recur: no control groups, self-reported attitudes, projects evaluating themselves, and too few validated instruments (hence the QTEdu Concept Inventory). For ages 9 to 14 in particular, a claim that some method "works" has no robust support. Feasibility and engagement have modest support, mostly from Einstein-First.

### Gaps
- I could not read the findings or inclusion criteria of the Yusuf et al. K-12 review or the PRPER 2024 review. These are the two most important sources to read next.
- I found no meta-analysis with pooled effect sizes for any age group.
