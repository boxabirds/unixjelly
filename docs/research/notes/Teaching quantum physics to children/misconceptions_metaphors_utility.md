# Misconceptions, mental models, metaphors and utility in teaching quantum physics (target age 9 to 14)

Scope note: almost all the misconception research is on upper-secondary (16 to 18), pre-service teachers or undergraduates. Very little is on ages 9 to 14. Each finding below says which age group it came from. Search budget was limited (about 15 calls), so several named authors (Kalkanis, Ireson, Müller & Wiesner) are reported through the 2017 review rather than from their original papers.

## (a) Documented misconceptions learners form

### Takeaway
The best-documented pattern is "misplaced classical thinking": learners keep classical pictures (tiny balls on definite paths, the planetary atom, uncertainty as measurement error) and bolt quantum words onto them. This is strongly documented for 16+ and undergraduates; for 9 to 14 there is almost no direct evidence, only the inference that younger learners would start from the same classical intuitions.

### Cited Findings
- The main review is Krijtenburg-Lewerissa, Pol, Brinkman and van Joolingen (2017), *Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res.* 13, 010109. It analyses 74 articles on secondary and lower-undergraduate quantum teaching — [arXiv 1701.01472](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472) (also [U. Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/insights-into-teaching-quantum-mechanics-in-secondary-and-lower-u/))
- Its overall conclusion is that students cannot connect quantum behaviour to physical reality as they see it, which leads them to mix classical and quantum concepts. The evidence is mainly undergraduate, but the review reports similarities at secondary level for duality and atomic models — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **Three clusters of wave-particle thinking** (Ireson; Ayene et al.; mainly undergraduate and pre-university). (1) "Classical": the object is only a particle or only a wave. (2) "Mixed": both behaviours coexist, but a single object is still described classically. (3) "Quasi-quantum": both behaviours are accepted, but events are still not described as non-deterministic. Students sit on a spectrum from classical to quantum thinking — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **Electrons as tiny balls on definite paths.** Many secondary and undergraduate students see electrons only as particles, and photons as "bright spherical balls with a definite location or trajectory" — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **"Particle or wave depending on other factors" (switching).** Listed under "mixed description": "Electrons are either a particle or a wave depending on other factors". Listed under "quasi-quantum": "Electrons or photons are waves and particles simultaneously" — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017, Table II](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **The wave as a wiggly path.** Some secondary and undergraduate students treat the electron wave as a pilot wave that forces the electron along a sinusoidal path (McKagan et al.; Olsen). Müller and Wiesner found students reading the wave as "a cloud of smeared charge" — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- Olsen found students accept both behaviours more easily for light than for electrons. Sen attributes better photon understanding to photons being introduced later in the curriculum, so with fewer prior misconceptions. That is suggestive evidence that the order of teaching matters — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **Double slit.** Some secondary students think photons deflect at the slit edges and then travel in straight lines to the screen — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **Measurement as clumsy disturbance.** Ayene et al. (tertiary) found four categories for the uncertainty principle: error from external effects, instrument error, measurement disturbance, and (correct) an intrinsic property. "Only a small number" were in the correct category. Müller and Wiesner and Singh saw the same at secondary and undergraduate level; some students said uncertainty comes from the particles' high speed — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **The planetary (Bohr) atom lock-in.** "Secondary and lower undergraduate students have difficulty letting go of Bohr's planetary atomic model" in the majority of the reviewed articles. Kalkanis et al. attribute this to students believing that what they learned earlier is scientifically correct. Stefani and Tsaparlis found models seen "as replicas of reality" — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- Taber: several secondary students did not see why quantisation was needed, "because they did not see the planetary model as insufficient". Some confused orbitals with planetary orbits or concentric shells — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- A study of 225 Greek grade 10 and 11 students found a Bohr-based model was the dominant mental model of atomic structure — [Chem. Educ. Res. Pract. 2017](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2017/rp/c7rp00135e)
- A Swedish study (2009) reported that the Bohr-derived atom taught in schools gave students an oversized, immobile nucleus and reinforced a planetary picture — [SSOAR record (Adbo et al.)](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/13430)
- **Spin as literal rotation.** Many students believe spin is an object spinning on its axis or around the nucleus. Özcan links this to holding the classical atom model — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **Superposition as ignorance (a mixture).** Students can compute probabilities from a superposition but fail to see that a superposition and a "lack of knowledge" mixture give different experimental results (Passante, Emigh and Shaffer; undergraduate and early graduate) — [Passante et al., PRST-PER 11, 020135 (2015)](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.11.020135)
- Superposition is difficult even for sophomore, junior and graduate students; an interactive lecture tutorial improved understanding — [PRST-PER 11, 020135](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.11.020135)
- Marshman and Singh (2015) frame upper-level quantum difficulties as analogous to introductory students' difficulties in classical mechanics. Common problems are distinguishing closely related concepts and making sense of the formalism — [Marshman & Singh 2015, arXiv 1504.02056](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.02056v2)
- **Styer (1996)** lists 15 misconceptions, mostly about the formalism (for example "energy eigenstates are the only allowed states", "the wavefunction is dimensionless"). He says their origins are speculation — [Styer, Am. J. Phys. 64, 31 (1996), PDF](https://tristan.nfshost.com/QM1/X/QuantumMisconceptions.pdf). A later source notes that "how common these misconceptions are is not clear" — [UvA thesis chapter](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/1478445/90951_05.pdf). Relevance to ages 9 to 14 is low, because the list targets university formalism.
- **Quantum computers as "try every answer in parallel".** Aaronson calls this "arguably the central layperson misconception". Measurement gives random outcomes, so any speed-up comes from interference: making wrong answers cancel. That helps for a few special problems such as factoring, not for NP-complete search in general — [Aaronson, "Speaking truth to parallelism"](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=201); quoted in [arXiv 2212.10734](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.10734)
- Quantum-computing misconceptions (university physics students): some claim a single qubit can be entangled; some think an N-qubit computer needs 2^N items rather than N — [Investigating and improving student understanding of the basics of quantum computing (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10597798-investigating-improving-student-understanding-basics-quantum-computing)
- A high-school curricula review warns that unvetted online materials spread misconceptions and overstate what quantum devices can do — [arXiv 2505.14809](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14809v1)
- Caveat on the word "misconception": Taber argues that some items labelled misconceptions are better called learning difficulties — [science-education-research.com](https://science-education-research.com/?p=32916)

### Inferences
- For a guide aimed at 9 to 14, the highest-risk misconceptions are the ones children can form from popular culture before any teaching: tiny balls on orbits (from school atom pictures and logos), "both at once" coins and cats, "quantum = magic/faster", and spin as spinning.
- Sen's observation that photons are understood better because they are met later implies that what children see first anchors them. That supports avoiding the planetary picture rather than teaching it and correcting it later. This is weak, correlational evidence.
- "Entanglement as faster-than-light signalling" and "quantum as mystical" are widely cited by experts, but I found no physics-education study that measures how common they are among school pupils.

### Gaps
- No misconception studies found specifically for ages 9 to 14 in quantum topics.
- Did not retrieve the original Kalkanis et al. (2003, *Sci. Educ.* 87, 257), Ireson (2000) or Müller & Wiesner (2002, *Am. J. Phys.* 70, 200) papers; findings come via the 2017 review.
- No prevalence data found for "entanglement allows faster-than-light messages" or "quantum is mystical" among school pupils.
- The full list of Styer's 15 items was not verified beyond the two quoted in his abstract.

## (b) Metaphors and analogies: which help, which harm

### Takeaway
The research consensus is cautious rather than experimental: classical metaphors are a known source of misconceptions, so any metaphor must have its limits stated explicitly. The only controlled comparison I found (a 2025 trial with 181 secondary students) favours photon polarisation and the double-well over a which-path single-photon approach. I found no controlled comparison of coin, cat, dice, socks or similar everyday analogies with any age group.

### Cited Findings
- Brookes and Etkina (2007) concluded that classical metaphors cause misconceptions and promote misplaced classical thinking. McKagan et al. observed the same over-literal reading (for example, potential wells seen as physical objects) — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472); original: Brookes & Etkina, PRST-PER 3, 010105
- The 2017 review's advice to teachers: "it is important to specify the limitations of metaphors, because they can lead to overliteral interpretations". It also says analogies should be well defined and diagrams unambiguous, but "the impact of these proposed solutions remains to be investigated" — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- Mixing diagrams (energy levels drawn with wave functions on top) was blamed for students confusing wave functions and energy levels (Domert et al.) — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **A liquid analogy that partly worked.** The Bremen "electronium" approach (Budde et al. 2002, upper secondary, n = 9) taught the atom as a continuous liquid-like substance. Some students still gave electronium a particle nature, but they did adopt the idea that electrons are not orbiting. The authors report high acceptance. Very small sample — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- **The one controlled comparison.** A cluster-randomised field trial (181 secondary students, *Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res.* 21(2), November 2025) compared three two-state approaches: which-path single photon, photon polarisation and double-well potential. All helped. Polarisation and double-well groups significantly outperformed the which-path group, whose students more often kept "mixed" thinking — [TU Dresden record](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/investigating-the-effect-of-twostate-approaches-on-students-understanding-of-quantum-measurement(9b767b56-1445-4c00-a451-ea4b4f8c67c6).html); [FAU summary](https://www.lightmatter.fau.de/2026/02/fau-lmq-research-spotlight-teaching-and-learning-quantum-physics-via-2-state-approaches-the-context-matters). Age of students not confirmed in what I read.
- Photon polarisation (the "Dirac approach") is proposed as a way to introduce quantum ideas from lower secondary (about 11 to 14) — [Univ. Udine, "From light polarization to quantum physics: supporting lower secondary school students' transition"](https://air.uniud.it/retrieve/51099da6-886c-4259-8fd5-423a4d8e61bc/from-light-polarization-to-quantum-physics-supporting-lower-secondary-school-students-transition-14587.pdf) (PDF blocked when fetched; only the search summary was seen)
- A secondary quantum-computing sequence built on polarisation leaves out complex numbers, matrices and programming — [EJMSTE, "A teaching approach to quantum computing at the secondary school level"](https://www.ejmste.com/download/a-teaching-approach-to-quantum-computing-at-the-secondary-school-level-17388.pdf)
- **Spinning coin.** Widely used, for example the APS "Tangled" game: while spinning, the coin "is not R or Y, but both" — [APS Tangled](https://www.aps.org/learning-resources/students/tangled). The criticism is that a coin's odds need only ordinary probabilities, while a qubit has amplitudes that can cancel, and interference is the whole point — [Bhatia blog, 2025 (opinion)](https://vidit-bhatia.github.io/blogs/2025/12/02/superposition-explained-qubits-not-coins/). Aaronson's core point backs this: amplitudes can cancel in a way probabilities cannot — [Aaronson blog](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=201). The coin is also used for decoherence (it eventually lands) — [Quantum Network knowledge base](https://www.quantum-network.com/knowledge-base/qne-quantum-superposition/)
- An older essay argues that classical analogies for superposition are "liable to be misleading", because quantum superposition requires indeterminate outcomes, which classical superposition does not — [Getting accustomed to the superposition principle (CSB/SJU)](https://faculty.csbsju.edu/frioux/super/MM-super.htm). Opinion, based on Dirac's argument.
- **Bell's (Bertlmann's) socks.** Bell introduced the socks (1981) to show what quantum correlations are *not*: socks have colours fixed in advance (local hidden variables), and entangled particles violate Bell's inequalities, which socks cannot — [arXiv 2308.03341](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.03341); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/amp/what-is-quantum-entanglement-2657446195). A *Physics Today* letter argues that the analogy misleads when used as an explanation, because spin is undefined until measured — [Physics Today letters](https://physicstoday.aip.org/letters/readers-offer-their-own-magic-moments-with-john-bell-1760014567182). The same logic applies to the "pair of gloves" analogy.
- **Schrödinger's cat.** I found only opinion: a PR blog calls the cat and the spinning coin journalists' default and the cat "useless" for newcomers — [Tyto PR blog (opinion)](https://tytopr.com/blog/communicating-quantum-the-cat-is-dead-guys). *Physics World* argues that bad metaphors make devices like "quantum telephones" or teleportation of people seem more plausible than they are — [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/lets-talk-about-quantum-2-0-why-we-need-to-sharpen-up-our-language/)
- The Einsteinian Physics programme (UWA) used the same models and analogies across ages 11 to 16 and found them effective at every age — [Kaur et al., arXiv 1707.03729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729). The programme is mostly gravity and relativity; quantum content (photons) is a minority.

### Inferences
- Bell designed the socks to *contrast* with quantum behaviour, not to explain it. If a guide uses socks or gloves, the faithful use is "this is what it would be like if the answers were fixed in advance — and experiments show it is not like this".
- The coin works for "you get a random answer when you look", but breaks exactly where quantum computing gets its power (interference). A guide that wants children to understand why quantum computers are not just fast computers should not rely on the coin alone.
- Polarising filters are the analogy-free option with the best evidence. They are real, cheap and safe, and children can predict and test them. The two-state approach also avoids the planetary atom and the wave/particle "switching" story entirely.

### Gaps
- No experimental comparison found for coin, cat, dice, spinning tops, water waves or blurred photographs as analogies, at any age.
- No study found on how 9 to 14 year olds interpret any specific quantum metaphor.
- I did not retrieve the 2025 two-state trial's full text (student ages, effect sizes).

## (c) Do early simplified models cause later harm? (Bohr as stepping stone or barrier)

### Takeaway
The evidence shows that the planetary/Bohr model is persistent and that students treat earlier models as true copies of reality. That is a real cost. But some experts argue the Bohr model should still be taught, as one model among several, with explicit comparison. No study directly tests "teach it right first" against "simplify then correct" in young children.

### Cited Findings
- Bohr persistence across the majority of the reviewed studies; Kalkanis et al. ascribe it to students believing previously learned content is correct — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- Kalkanis et al. (98 pre-service teachers) contrasted classical and quantum models of hydrogen explicitly, using the uncertainty relation as the basic principle. A "vast majority" then described the atom correctly — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- McKagan, Perkins and Wieman, "Why we should teach the Bohr model and how to teach it effectively" (PRST-PER 4, 010103, 2008). Their course emphasised the predictions of each model and the reasons each was replaced, and this increased use of the Schrödinger model (undergraduate). They also found students struggle to see why new models were needed — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- More experienced students understood the differences between models and could switch between them (Ke et al.; Wang and Barrow) — [Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)
- Upper-secondary students "cannot easily move beyond" planetary and Bohr models, probably because they rarely feel the need for new ones — [Univ. Padova, *Education Sciences* 14, 239](https://research.unipd.it/retrieve/1151c453-a870-4082-897b-2956adc91c52/education-14-00239.pdf)
- Long-term retention exists for early modern-physics teaching. UWA taught Einsteinian physics to a class aged 10 to 11 in 2011; 11 of 26 were followed up in 2020 and still remembered key concepts such as curved space-time. Small sample, and mostly not quantum — [UWA research repository](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/long-term-impact-of-a-primary-school-intervention-on-aspects-of-e/)

### Inferences
- Two lessons for a children's guide: (1) if an orbit picture is shown at all, label it as an old model that was replaced and say why; (2) better, start from experiments children can do (filters) rather than from an atom picture.
- The "lies-to-children" defence (simplify now, correct later) relies on a later correction happening. For most 9 to 14 year olds who never study quantum physics formally, there is no later correction, so the simplification becomes the permanent belief. This is my inference, not a finding.

### Gaps
- No direct study of whether early simplified quantum models harm or help later learning in children.
- I did not read McKagan et al. 2008 in full; its argument is reported via the review.

## (d) Utility: what can a 9 to 14 year old realistically DO afterwards?

### Takeaway
Evidence of what children can do is thin and mostly self-evaluated by programme providers. Defensible outcomes for 9 to 14: describe the quantum atom without orbits, say that electrons behave like waves, predict polariser results, and explain that a measurement gives a random answer with predictable odds. Quantum key distribution and quantum circuits have been taught at secondary level, mostly to older students. Learning-outcome frameworks exist (the European CFQT) but are pitched at professional and higher education.

### Cited Findings
- FLEET (Australia) primary workshops, Years 5 and 6 (about 10 to 12), used role-play and pencil-graphite circuits. Their own 2023 pre/post evaluations report that students could conceptualise the quantum model of an atom and understood that electrons behave like waves. Provider self-evaluation; no independent check — [FLEET case study](https://www.fleet.org.au/case_studies/primary-school-quantum-and-year-10-future-electronics/); [FLEET pilot](https://www.fleet.org.au/?p=485); [Why teach primary students quantum physics?](https://archive.fleet.org.au/blog/why-teach-primary-students-quantum-physics/)
- Einsteinian Physics (UWA), ages 11 to 16. Year 9 (n = 57, ten weeks) average class scores rose from 23% to 91%. Year 10 (n = 28, four lessons) and Year 11 (n = 27, one day; improvement factor 2.1) also improved. Pre-tests showed very little prior knowledge at every age. Provider-run, no control groups — [Kaur et al., arXiv 1707.03729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729)
- Year 6 students (about 11) in an eight-week programme mostly found it interesting; only one called it boring. The authors concluded Year 6 were "conceivably ready" — [Kaur et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729)
- Polarisation approach for lower secondary: predicting filter outcomes and single-photon probabilities is the core activity — [Univ. Udine paper](https://air.uniud.it/retrieve/51099da6-886c-4259-8fd5-423a4d8e61bc/from-light-polarization-to-quantum-physics-supporting-lower-secondary-school-students-transition-14587.pdf) (summary only; full text not read)
- Secondary quantum literacy through quantum computing and quantum key distribution: positive trends in familiarity and in understanding of superposition and entanglement after a presentation — [MDPI *Education Sciences* 15(9), 1167](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/9/1167). Age not confirmed.
- Hands-on quantum computing workshop for high-school-aged youth: significant pre/post gain in basic knowledge — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356206341_Teaching_Quantum_Computing_to_High-School-Aged_Youth_A_Hands-On_Approach)
- A five-day university outreach programme for high school students showed large learning gains across classical physics, quantum physics and quantum computing, equitable regardless of prior physics, chemistry, computer science or maths background — [EPJ Quantum Technology (2026)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjqt/s40507-026-00515-w)
- East African outreach: quantum lab kits made from marbles and balloons reached nearly 4,000 children; no formal outcome data in the excerpt seen — [APS News, Oct 2026](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2026/10/east-african-kids-quantum-physics)
- **Explaining why quantum computers are not just fast computers**: the correct explanation (interference, not trying everything) is set out in [Aaronson](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=201) and [Ferrie, "Myth 3"](https://csferrie.medium.com/myth-3-quantum-computers-try-all-solutions-at-once-f96bcd7d6d7a) (expert opinion). No study found of children reaching this outcome.
- **Frameworks.** The European Competence Framework for Quantum Technologies (CFQT, QTEdu / Quantum Flagship) has 8 domains, 42 sub-domains and proficiency levels A1 (awareness) to C2 (innovation). It was built from a three-round Delphi study and is aimed at courses, degrees and industry — [CFQT v2.5 PDF](https://qt.eu/media/pdf/Competence_Framework_for_QT_v2_5_2024.pdf); [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/15209861); [arXiv 2308.10371](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.10371v2). I found no school-age (9 to 14) learning-outcome framework.

### Inferences
- Realistic "can do" outcomes for 9 to 14, supported by the polarisation and FLEET work: predict what happens with two or three polarising filters (including the surprise third filter); say that one photon either passes or does not, and only the odds are predictable; describe an atom without orbits.
- Plausible but unevidenced for this age: explaining the eavesdropper-detection idea behind quantum key distribution; building a one- or two-gate quantum circuit in a simulator; spotting hype in "quantum" product claims. These are reasonable design goals, but no study shows 9 to 14 year olds achieving them.
- The "read news critically" outcome matches the documented hype problem ([arXiv 2505.14809](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14809v1); [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/lets-talk-about-quantum-2-0-why-we-need-to-sharpen-up-our-language/)) and is probably the most transferable everyday skill. This is my inference.

### Gaps
- No independent, controlled evaluation of a quantum course for ages 9 to 14 was found.
- No study of children building quantum circuits or doing QKD at 9 to 14 was found.
- No school-level learning-outcome framework was found; CFQT A1 may serve as a reference but is not designed for children.

## (e) Attitude and identity effects, especially for girls

### Takeaway
One research group (UWA Einsteinian Physics) reports that girls start lower and gain more in interest and understanding, replicated across all its own programmes for ages 11 to 16. The evidence is promising but provider-run, small and mostly about relativity rather than quantum physics. No quantum-specific gender study for ages 9 to 14 was found.

### Cited Findings
- Year 9, twenty lessons (2014): boys had slightly higher interest throughout, but girls showed a significantly greater increase in "I think physics is an interesting subject" — [Kaur et al., arXiv 1707.03729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729)
- Year 10, four lessons (2015): girls scored lowest on the conceptual pre-test but exceeded boys on the post-test — [Kaur et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729)
- The authors say the greater improvement for girls "has been replicated in all our studies". They suggest a reason: the current curriculum is based on old-fashioned concepts. They propose that conceptual modern physics may encourage gender balance in STEM — [Kaur et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729)
- Häussler and Hoffmann (2002, *J. Res. Sci. Teach.*): a year-long programme for seventh graders (about 12 to 13), with curriculum changes and teacher support for girls' physics self-concept, improved most indicators for girls (and boys). General physics, not quantum — summarised in search results; original not fetched (see [arXiv 1707.03729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729) context)
- High-school quantum information outreach: a study of student attitudes towards quantum information science and technology — [Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res. 20, 020126 (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.20.020126) (content not retrieved)
- A case study of secondary students' views on the relevance of learning quantum physics reports gender differences, but I did not see their direction — [arXiv 2001.10840](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10840)
- Qubit by Qubit's "Quantum Quest" camp for young women is a conference abstract with limited evidence — [APS March Meeting 2023](https://meetings-archive.aps.org/mar/2023/aaa01/4)
- A 2022 scoping review of 215 STEM interest interventions for secondary students, focused on girls — [PMC9593045](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9593045)
- A short intervention on gravitational waves and quantum physics was evaluated for understanding and attitude — [arXiv 1807.02995](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.02995) (content not retrieved)

### Inferences
- The UWA finding is the strongest available signal, but it is one group evaluating its own work without control groups. Treat it as a hypothesis worth designing for, not a result to cite as established.
- Novelty may explain part of the attitude gains (any new, engaging topic tends to raise interest in short programmes). None of the studies seen controls for this.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found of quantum-specific exposure and girls' interest at ages 9 to 14.
- No long-term identity or subject-choice data found beyond the 11-of-26 UWA follow-up.
