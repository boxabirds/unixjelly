# Start with filters, not atoms or history

The best-supported way to teach quantum physics to children aged about 9 to 14 is to choose one simple two-state system, ideally the polarisation of light, and teach four ideas on it, with no maths beyond angles and counting. The four ideas are: a measurement gives a random answer; the odds of each answer can be predicted; two possibilities can cancel each other out (interference); and measuring changes the thing measured. Two expert panels now put interference, superposition and measurement at the core, taught "at a conceptual rather than a mathematical level" ([Bitzenbauer et al. 2025](https://journals.aps.org/prper/abstract/10.1103/d1yx-kfsz)). The one randomised comparison of starting points favours polarisation over the popular "which path did the photon take" set-up ([Tóth et al. 2025, FAU summary](https://www.lightmatter.fau.de/2026/02/fau-lmq-research-spotlight-teaching-and-learning-quantum-physics-via-2-state-approaches-the-context-matters)). Black-body radiation, the Bohr orbit, wave equations, complex numbers and matrices can all be left out or replaced. But be clear about the evidence. **I found no controlled study of any quantum course for children under about 14.** Every headline result comes from older or self-selected pupils, from programmes that evaluated themselves, or from attitude surveys. The famous claims also shrink when you read the papers. Quantum Picturalism's "high schoolers beat Oxford" pilot used volunteers aged 16 to 18, had no control group, and compared no scores with Oxford students ([arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)). For a Jelly guide, this means two things. The design can rest on sound principles: a polariser spine, predict before you run, one picture for a state, and guarded hints. But whether it works for 9 to 11 year olds is an open question, and only watching children will answer it.

A note on sources. This report comes from four sets of research notes gathered on 28 October 2026 with a limited search budget. Many primary papers were read only as abstracts or through a 2017 review. Where that matters, the text says so. "Evidence" below means a measured result. "Opinion" means expert argument or a programme's own claims. "Inference" means my reasoning from the evidence.

## Most of the traditional prerequisites are history, not physics

School curricula teach quantum physics in historical order. A study of upper-secondary curricula in 15 countries found the same seven shared topics: energy levels in atoms, light and matter, wave–particle duality, the de Broglie wavelength, applications, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, and probability ([Stadermann et al. 2019](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.15.010130)). Expert opinion is moving away from that order. A 2019 expert panel (a Delphi study, where experts rank topics over several rounds) put duality, wave functions and atoms first ([Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2019](https://utrecht-main-test.atmire.com/items/12982779-7b63-4621-96a1-f45ac7e9b4df)). A 2025 panel, reportedly of 175 experts ([CERN Indico](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1175859/contributions/6400786/)), put **interference, superposition and measurement** first, and judged advanced quantum-technology topics unsuitable for school ([Bitzenbauer et al. 2025](https://journals.aps.org/prper/abstract/10.1103/d1yx-kfsz)). The panels and questions were different, so treat this as a direction of travel, not a finding. Nobody has run a head-to-head trial of "quantum first" against "history first" at any age.

The table sorts the layers the brief named. The verdicts are my inferences from the evidence cited in the text. They are not tested rules.

| Layer | Verdict for ages 9 to 14 | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Waves (water, sound, wave equations) | Replace | Interference is the idea that matters, and rotating arrows or a two-path set-up give it without wave maths |
| Probability | Teach just in time, as counting | Needed for every outcome. Fractions of clicks out of 10 or 100 may be the real barrier at 9 to 11 |
| Energy and energy levels | Skip, unless the course covers spectra, lasers or chemistry | No part of the four-idea core depends on it |
| Light | Teach just in time: "light comes in lumps" | Photons are the youngest evidenced idea (about age 11) |
| Atoms and the Bohr model | Skip, or show only as "an old model that was replaced" | The planetary picture is persistent and hard to remove |
| Measurement | Core: teach early, on the chosen system | The centre of both the polariser and the key-sharing routes |
| Complex numbers | Replace with arrows or angles | Feynman-style arrows and Quantum Picturalism both remove them |
| Linear algebra | Replace with diagrams | Quantum Picturalism needs only adding angles in degrees |

Successful programmes all scope the same way. They pick one narrow physical system and one way of drawing it, and leave out anything that drawing doesn't need. **Quantum Picturalism** replaces matrices, tensors, complex numbers and trigonometry with diagram-rewriting rules. Its authors say the only maths needed is "the ability to express angles in degrees and add them together" ([arXiv 2512.00141](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.00141v1)). **Einstein-First** in Western Australia rejects "the belief that to understand modern physics students need to have a strong background in classical" physics, and uses physical models instead, such as toy foam-dart guns for photons ([Kaur et al., Part 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729); [Part 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03728)). **Spin First** in Germany uses electron spin as its one two-state system for 14 to 15 year olds ([EPJ Quantum Technology 2025](https://epjqt.epj.org/articles/epjqt/abs/2025/01/40507_2025_Article_436/40507_2025_Article_436.html)). **Quantum key distribution** (QKD: sharing a secret key so that any eavesdropper leaves traces) needs only polarisation, a choice of filter angle, and probability. It has unplugged versions with coins and boxes ([Waterloo IQC "Quantum Coins"](https://uwaterloo.ca/institute-for-quantum-computing/sites/default/files/uploads/files/02-qcrypto-coins-student.pdf)).

Each replacement costs something. Probability games with coins or dice cannot show interference, because ordinary probabilities never cancel. A course built only on coins teaches randomness and measurement but misses what is truly quantum. Arrows are the cheapest stand-in that keeps cancellation. A sum-over-paths (Feynman arrows) sequence "may be effective" for building consistent mental models, in a group of just 14 Italian students ([Malgieri et al. 2017](https://doaj.org/article/713f6331c84240c085a755449682909b)). Tactile "phasor wheels", physical dials for adding arrows, were reportedly tried with 14 to 15 year olds ([ECU 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ecu2021-09314)). Both are weak evidence. Every substitute loses continuous wave functions, atoms, and an easy path to standard notation. No study has measured whether learners pay for that later.

The choice of system matters as much as history versus no history. In a cluster-randomised trial (whole classes randomly assigned) with 181 secondary students, all three two-state approaches helped. Polarisation and the double well beat the which-path single photon. The which-path students more often kept a mixed classical and quantum picture ([Tóth et al. 2025, TU Dresden record](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/investigating-the-effect-of-twostate-approaches-on-students-understanding-of-quantum-measurement(9b767b56-1445-4c00-a451-ea4b4f8c67c6).html)). I read only summaries, so I have no effect sizes and no confirmed ages. It is still the strongest single piece of evidence for choosing where to start.

What about age? The evidence suggests a rough floor for each idea, with the warning that every point is weak. Photons as particles: about age 11 (Einstein-First, pooled with relativity). Superposition and measurement on a two-state system: about 14 (Spin First, about 11 interviews). Interference with arrows: about 14 to 15 (weak). Entanglement: about 16 (Quantum Picturalism, a pilot). **For 9 to 13 year olds, no quantum idea has been tested in a controlled study.** Proportional reasoning ("3 clicks out of 10 is the same odds as 30 out of 100") is the likeliest real barrier at 9 to 11. No quantum-specific study tests it.

## Named programmes deliver engagement; learning gains remain unproven

The evidence base is small, mostly self-evaluated and nearly silent on under-14s. The best K-12 review calls itself "an early systematic review" ([Yusuf et al., Computer Science Education](https://portalcientifico.uned.es/documentos/68b33d4e2f359d6e8f723e6d?lang=en)). I could not read its findings. A 2022 conference session said "a thorough evaluation with reliable and validated instruments is still missing" for two-state approaches ([GIREP 2022](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1092708/sessions/443681/)). A European project is still building the first agreed test of secondary pupils' quantum understanding, called a concept inventory ([Utrecht](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/community-based-development-of-the-quantum-concept-inventory-in-c/)). With no agreed test, no study can be compared with another.

**Quantum Picturalism (Oxford and Quantinuum)** is the most cited and the most over-sold. The 2023 pilot recruited 75 volunteers and analysed 54, without explaining the 21 who are missing. They were aged 16 to 18 (mean 16.84) and were taught in weekly online tutorials over eight weeks. The design was a single test at the end, with no control group ([arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1)). The mean mark was 57.9%, with a wide spread. Raw marks gave 20 distinctions and 15 fails. The headline "82% passed, 48% distinction" depends on a rule about two "canary" questions. The two markers invented the method, and one marked systematically higher than the other (t(53) = 4.57). Most authors work for Quantinuum, which sells products built on this maths ([arXiv 2504.01013](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01013v1); [arXiv 2512.00141](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.00141)). The exam was "usually given to" Oxford postgraduates. No Oxford students sat it for comparison, so "beat Oxford" is press framing ([The Quantum Insider](https://thequantuminsider.com/?p=2377047)). The fair reading is this: pictures let some motivated teenagers do real quantum-circuit reasoning without advanced maths. It does not show that pictures beat standard teaching, and it says nothing direct about 9 to 14 year olds.

**Einstein-First (University of Western Australia)** has the most research that actually covers the target ages. Its quantum strand is a small part of a mostly relativity and gravity course. Year 9 pupils selected as academically talented (n = 57, 20 lessons) went from a class average of 23% to 91%. A Year 6 class (n = 26) gave only attitude data ([Kaur et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729)). A one-day programme for Years 7 to 10 taught core ideas regardless of age or prior knowledge. Short programmes did not reach the deeper ideas that build on the core ones ([Einstein-First papers list](https://www.einsteinianphysics.com/published-papers/); [Choudhary et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342665173_A_Comparison_of_Short_and_Long_Einsteinian_Physics_Intervention_Programmes_in_Middle_School)). The finding repeated most often is about girls. They start with lower interest and scores and catch up or overtake by the end. The authors say this "has been replicated in all our studies" ([Kaur et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03729); [gender response paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06323)). This is the most promising signal in the field for 10 to 14 year olds. It is also one team evaluating itself without control groups, and novelty effects are not ruled out. Its quantum-specific modules for ages 11 to 15 (spin, phasors, quantum probability) report enjoyment or "successfully trialled", with no numbers.

The **younger-age studies** are thin. A US 6th-grade study (n = 201) folded wave–particle duality and energy quantisation into ordinary lessons. Scores rose from 2.0 to 3.4 out of 9, with no control group ([Kim and Menekse, ASEE 2026](https://nemo.asee.org/public/conferences/374/papers/51374/view)). The gain is real but modest, and the topic is light, not qubits. Australia's FLEET centre ran primary workshops for Years 5 and 6. Its own evaluation reports that children could picture the quantum atom and knew electrons behave like waves ([FLEET](https://www.fleet.org.au/case_studies/primary-school-quantum-and-year-10-future-electronics/)). Spin First interviewed about 11 pupils aged 14 to 15. They found qualitative understanding without much mixing of classical and quantum ideas, but also trouble with language and with "hidden variable" reasoning, where the answer is assumed to be fixed before you look ([EPJ QT 2025](https://epjqt.epj.org/articles/epjqt/abs/2025/01/40507_2025_Article_436/40507_2025_Article_436.html)).

**Programmes that start with quantum computing** have large reach and almost no outcome data. Qubit by Qubit reports thousands of students in conference abstracts, without pre/post figures ([APS 2021](https://archive.aps.org/mar/2021/a29/9); [APS 2023](https://archive.aps.org/mar/2023/k34/11)). Q-CTRL's Black Opal figures are a company case study of undergraduates' self-reports ([Q-CTRL](https://q-ctrl.com/case-study/improving-quantum-education-outcomes-with-black-opal-at-the-university-of-hull-uk)). QuanTime, the US "Hour of Code"-style quantum activities, is the most relevant to the target ages. Its only evaluation surveyed teachers: 36 of 132 responded, 12 of them teaching grades 4 to 8. The authors say it "did not assess student understanding or learning outcomes" ([arXiv 2510.15785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15785)). Teachers of younger pupils preferred the art and polarisation activity. That is a small hint in the same direction as the Tóth trial. For IBM's Qiskit material, Microsoft's Quantum Katas, Quirk, Perimeter Institute resources and Quantum Quest, I found no published evaluation. That means "not found", not "does not exist".

## Classical pictures stick, so choose them as contrasts

The best-documented failure is "misplaced classical thinking". Learners keep a classical picture and stick quantum words onto it. A review of 74 studies found three levels of wave–particle thinking. "Classical": it is only a particle or only a wave. "Mixed": it switches between the two "depending on other factors". "Quasi-quantum": it is both, but still behaves predictably ([Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)). Students picture photons as "bright spherical balls with a definite location or trajectory". They read the electron's wave as a wiggly path. They treat uncertainty as clumsy measurement or instrument error rather than a property of nature. They take spin to mean literal spinning ([same review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)). Nearly all of this evidence comes from pupils aged 16 or over and from undergraduates. **No misconception study for ages 9 to 14 was found.** The inference is that younger children start from the same intuitions, plus whatever popular culture has already given them.

**The planetary atom is the clearest case of an early model doing lasting harm.** In most of the reviewed studies, students could not let go of Bohr's orbits. One explanation is that they believe what they learned first is correct. Another is that they see models "as replicas of reality" ([Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472)). Among 225 Greek 15 to 17 year olds, a Bohr-based model was the dominant picture of the atom ([Chem. Educ. Res. Pract. 2017](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2017/rp/c7rp00135e)). The other side deserves a fair hearing. McKagan, Perkins and Wieman argue that the evidence that "Bohr inhibits learning" is weak. They say the model should be taught as a model and compared openly with the modern one, which worked with undergraduates ([arXiv 0707.1541](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/0707.1541)). Both arguments assume a later course where the correction happens. Most 9 to 14 year olds will never take one. For them, a simplified picture becomes the permanent belief. That is my inference, but it is a strong reason to avoid the orbit picture rather than plan to fix it later.

Metaphors have almost no experimental evidence either way. The research advice is cautious: classical metaphors cause misconceptions, so "it is important to specify the limitations of metaphors" ([Krijtenburg-Lewerissa et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.01472), citing Brookes and Etkina 2007). **The spinning coin** is everywhere, for example in the APS game Tangled, where the spinning coin "is not R or Y, but both" ([APS](https://www.aps.org/learning-resources/students/tangled)). It works for "you get a random answer when you look". It fails exactly where quantum computers get their power. Scott Aaronson's central point is that quantum amplitudes can cancel, which ordinary probabilities cannot ([Aaronson](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=201)). **Bell's socks** (or a pair of gloves) were introduced by Bell to show what entanglement is *not*. Socks have their colours fixed in advance, and real entangled particles break the rule that fixed-in-advance answers must obey ([arXiv 2308.03341](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.03341)). Used as an explanation, socks teach the exact hidden-variable idea that Spin First found in 14 year olds. **Schrödinger's cat** has only opinion against it ([Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/lets-talk-about-quantum-2-0-why-we-need-to-sharpen-up-our-language/)). Polarising filters avoid the problem, because they are not a metaphor. They are the real thing, and children can predict and test them.

Superposition is also easily confused with plain ignorance. Even graduate students can compute odds from a superposition but miss that it gives different results from a 50/50 mix of known states ([Passante et al. 2015](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.11.020135)). The other popular error is that quantum computers "try every answer at once". Aaronson calls this "arguably the central layperson misconception": any speed-up comes from making wrong answers cancel, and only for special problems ([Aaronson](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=201); [Ferrie](https://csferrie.medium.com/myth-3-quantum-computers-try-all-solutions-at-once-f96bcd7d6d7a)).

What can a child realistically do afterwards? Going by the polarisation work and FLEET's self-evaluation, a defensible target looks like this. A child can predict what happens with two and then three polarising filters, including the surprise when a third filter lets light back through. A child can say that one photon either passes or doesn't, and that only the odds can be predicted. A child can describe an atom without orbits. Some further goals are plausible but have no evidence at this age. These are explaining how quantum key sharing catches an eavesdropper, building a one- or two-gate circuit, and spotting hype in "quantum" product claims. The last of these is probably the most useful everyday skill, given how much exaggerated material exists ([arXiv 2505.14809](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14809v1)). Entanglement belongs later. Even high-school volunteers in a game pilot found it harder than superposition ([Education Sciences 2022](https://air.uniud.it/retrieve/3291f9d3-c88d-4431-98cc-1d9e90d3ac03/education-12-00446-v2.pdf)).

## Formats that work: predict, run, explain, with hints that can't be skipped

No quantum simulation or game has learning evidence from children aged 9 to 14. What transfers is design, from university simulations and from general learning research. **QuVis** (St Andrews) is the closest precedent for the Jelly model. Each simulation has built-in challenges with a hint button. Learners explore known states before the unknown-state challenges. The interface uses "implicit scaffolding": limited controls that guide students "without them feeling guided". In one undergraduate class, 20 of 33 students did the full sequence. Correct answers on a superposition-versus-mixture question "substantially increased", with no control group ([Kohnle et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07905)). **Quantum Composer** (Aarhus) gives the main warning. In think-aloud interviews, it "helps students to obtain the correct answers", but they needed more support to develop the reasoning behind them ([Ahmed et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.13260)). A guided course can reward the game instead of the physics. The fix is to make children predict before they run anything and explain afterwards.

Games are a weak base for quantum specifically, but sound in general. A review by 23 authors of quantum games and tools (Hello Quantum, Quantum Flytrap's Virtual Lab, QPlayLearn and others) reported **no outcome data**; the tools were "described by their developers" ([Seskir et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07756v1)). The only study in the target age band, Quander (ages 10 to 13), used focus groups and found "various levels of understanding" ([SIGCSE 2026](https://sigcse2026.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2026-Papers/123/Quandray-Student-Conceptions-of-Quantum-Concepts-from-a-Gameworld)). The Quantum TiqTaqToe pilot taught the ideas *before* the game, and the tournament then helped link play to learning ([Education Sciences 2022](https://air.uniud.it/retrieve/3291f9d3-c88d-4431-98cc-1d9e90d3ac03/education-12-00446-v2.pdf)). That supports "explain, then play". Outside quantum physics the evidence is firmer. A meta-analysis of 57 comparisons found that games beat non-game teaching by an effect size of 0.33 (a small to moderate gain). Better-designed games beat standard versions by a similar margin, which points to "the key role of design beyond medium" ([Clark et al. 2016](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/YHX5ZVR8); [SRI brief](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/digital-learning-pubs/digital-games-design-and-learning-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-brief/)). Simpler visuals did as well as complex ones, or better ([Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=16779)).

Two findings bear directly on the Jelly mechanics. First, **worked examples with fading**. Show a fully worked step, then have the learner fill in more and more of it. This improves near transfer and cuts errors. Adaptive fading shows the step again after a failure ([Renkl et al. 2000](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.11780/196); [Moreno, ASU](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Stat_Adapt_Fad_JEE06.pdf)). Second, **hint abuse**. In a geometry tutor, students abused hints on 36% of actions (14% if rapid repeats count once), mostly by clicking through to the last hint, which gives the answer. Time spent reading hints went with better test scores. The number of hint requests went with worse ones ([LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse)). Those figures come from secondary summaries and are worth checking against the 2006 original. A "do it for me" button is that last, answer-giving hint. If a jelly can be earned by pressing it, some children will learn to press it. Neither finding comes from quantum physics or from 9 to 14 year olds. Both come from maths and engineering tutoring.

Put together, the evidence points to a first Jelly module with a polariser spine. Roughly: light passes or it doesn't; crossed filters block everything; light arrives in single clicks; at 45° each photon is a coin toss, so count 10 and then 100; the angle sets the odds; measuring changes the photon; the three-filter surprise, predicted first; the same question at new angles, first worked and then faded; two mystery boxes, one a 50/50 mix and one all at 45°, told apart by choosing the right filter (the QuVis challenge, simplified); one arrow picture for a state; a beam splitter; and two paths that cancel, so that one detector never clicks until a path is blocked. Only then comes a short quantum noughts and crosses game, and an "explain it to a friend" check that rewards reasoning. That sequence is mostly my design judgement. The evidence supports its parts, not the whole. Entanglement waits for a later module. The polariser model also needs a physicist's check. Following the Jelly rule "say when the sandbox differs", every simplified step should say where real light behaves differently.

The current Jelly mechanics fit well, with three guards. "Do it for me" should be followed by a near-identical step the child does alone before the jelly is awarded. Hints should escalate from a question, to a pointer, to the answer. Each step that runs an experiment should ask for a prediction first, so a correct click is not mistaken for understanding. The Jelly prerequisite system (`teaches`, `taught()` and the concept detectors) is a natural defence against the main risk this report identifies. It can stop "superposition", "photon", an orbit picture or a coin metaphor from appearing before the step that defines it. QKD fits well after Security Jelly 1, and after the planned Security Jelly 2's "who else can see your messages". Those courses already give children eavesdroppers and keys, so QKD would build on taught ideas rather than adding new ones. On its own, though, QKD never reaches interference. It works as a reward module after the core, not as the core.

## Conclusion

Most of the "prerequisite problem" turns out to be about curriculum history, not physics. The hard prerequisites for a correct first model are only counting, simple fractions and angles. The hard part is not the maths. It is stopping classical pictures from getting in first. That moves the risk from what to teach to what to show. For a course of short steps, a single orbit diagram, a coin described as "both at once" or the word "superposition" used before it is defined does more damage than a missing explanation. Children who never take another quantum course keep whatever they saw first. For the Jelly guides this is good news, because enforcing "never show a concept before it is taught" is already built into the code.

The uncomfortable part is that no one knows whether this works for 9 to 11 year olds. The lower age bound depends on proportional reasoning, which has never been tested in a quantum setting. The programmes with impressive numbers all used older, talented or self-selected learners, and evaluated themselves. A Jelly quantum course could not cite evidence that it teaches children quantum physics. It could honestly claim to follow the best-supported design. The cheapest way to find out is the one QuVis used: watch a few children work through each step one at a time, and record what they predict, explain and click past. Ten observation sessions would give more evidence for this age band than the published literature does today.
