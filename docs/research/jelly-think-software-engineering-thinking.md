# Teach children the machine, not the syntax

A child becomes a good software engineering thinker by learning a small, drawable model of what a computer does, not by collecting language features. That model is an exact executor working through instructions in order, changing named values over time, repeating, choosing, and calling named procedures that take inputs and return answers. Around it sit a handful of practices: predict and trace before you write, explain what code is for, break problems into named parts, write examples that double as tests, and debug by finding the first surprise. The best-evidenced teaching move is **reading and predicting code before writing it**. Its school-level support is real but small: the PRIMM trial with 493 pupils aged 11 to 14 found an effect of **r = .13** ([Sentance, Waite & Kallia 2019](https://suesentance.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/teaching_computer_programming_with_primm__a_sociocultural_perspective_author_copy.pdf)). Most other findings, including the strongest results on misconceptions, prediction, Parsons problems and worked examples, come from **university students**, and the report flags this wherever it applies. The "limits" curriculum (what is impossible, what is merely expensive, and what goes wrong when things happen at once) runs **ahead of every 9-to-14 curriculum found**; formal undecidability sits at age 16 and over. It should therefore be taught as intuition through sandbox experiences, not as proof. When AI writes code, what still matters is exactly this core plus judgement: deciding what to build, specifying it precisely with examples, reading and testing what comes back, knowing that tests cannot prove correctness, and treating language models as text predictors rather than minds. The recommended Think Jelly course is 22 missions in six arcs, with a core track for ages 9 to 11 and extensions for 12 to 14. It uses a pretend robot whose language is a **strict subset of JavaScript from the first lesson** and grows one rule at a time. Its final arc teaches functions as handlers that "the world calls", which is the exact shape Server Jelly needs.

## A drawable machine and nine constructs make the minimal core

### The notional machine is the real subject of a language-free course

Du Boulay coined "notional machine" for "the idealised model of the computer implied by the constructs of the programming language". Sorva argues that this model should be **taught explicitly** rather than left for learners to infer ([arXiv 2510.16594, summarising du Boulay and Sorva](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.16594v1)). The claim rests mainly on university studies, but the reason it matters is easy to see in them. At the end of a first-year Java course, about **one third of students held non-viable models of value assignment and only about 17% held a viable model of reference assignment**. Students with viable models did significantly better on coursework and exams ([Ma et al. 2007](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/3145/)). Program visualisation helps build the model, but the 2013 review of these tools found the evidence "insufficient for drawing more nuanced conclusions with respect to learner engagement" ([Sorva, Karavirta & Malmi 2013](https://romisatriawahono.net/lecture/rm/survey/software%20engineering/Software%20Architecture/Sorva%20-%20Generic%20Program%20Visualization%20Systems%20for%20Introductory%20Programming%20-%202013.pdf)). A 2026 review notes that experiments which vary *what the learner does* (viewing, responding, changing, constructing) produce significant effects far more reliably than experiments which vary *how the state is drawn* ([arXiv 2606.19570](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19570)). Tracing is also a developmental marker. In think-aloud studies of university novices, some could not trace reliably at all; others could trace but could not say what the code was *for*; only the most advanced reasoned deductively from the code itself ([Teague & Lister 2014](https://opus.cloud1.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/33413)). In the multinational Lister study, students who scribbled variable values on the paper as they traced did better than those who left the page blank ([Lister et al. 2004](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)).

The implication for a course that refuses to be "about a language" is that the machine model *is* the curriculum. One picture should be reused everywhere: named boxes for values, a "you are here" pointer for control, a stack of cards for procedure calls and, much later, arrows for references. Children should be asked to predict what that picture will look like next rather than simply watch it animate. No randomised study of notional-machine teaching with 9-to-14-year-olds was found. The only child-specific work is a small thesis on a Hedy visualisation tool tested with primary pupils ([Hedy PV tool thesis 2022](https://www.hedy.org/research/An_Interactive_Program_Visualisation_Tool_for_Hedy_2022.pdf)).

### Children's misconceptions come from English, maths and a "hidden mind"

The oldest language-independent account is still the most useful. Pea described three bugs, parallelism, intentionality and egocentrism, that occur "from primary school to college age". He traced them to one **"superbug": the assumption that "there is a hidden mind somewhere in the programming language that has intelligent interpretive powers"** ([Pea 1986](https://telearn.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00190538)). A later refinement argues that the problem is attributing the *wrong* capacities to the computer, not attributing mental states at all. That matters for a course built around a friendly robot character ([Larghi & Datteri 2025](https://www.boa.unimib.it/retrieve/889eb162-5687-4a83-9208-90aed942813d/Larghi-2025-ATEESpring2024-VoR.pdf)).

Two child studies give the clearest picture for this age range. Swidan, Hermans and Smit tested **145 learners aged 7 to 17** on 11 known misconceptions using Scratch. The three most common were difficulty with the **sequential order of statements**, the belief that **a variable can hold more than one value**, and the belief that **a program is interactive whenever input is mentioned**. Misconceptions were associated with the maths meaning of numbers and operators, with the everyday meaning of identifier names, and with high expectations of what a computer can do. Older children did better on variables and control ([Swidan, Hermans & Smit 2018](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_44464443-cbe7-4591-a3e3-b51ca04cb907?preview=1)). Grover and Basu studied **100 pupils in grades 6 to 8** after an introductory Scratch course. They found the pupils "generally unfamiliar with the use of variables" and confused about how loops execute and how Boolean operators work ([Grover & Basu 2017](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/measuring-student-learning-in-introductory-block-based-programming-examining-misconceptions-of-loops-variables-and-boolean-logic/)). Younger children show the language effect directly: pupils with a mean age of 8.65 **ran Scratch "repeat" loops one extra time**, probably reading "repeat" as "do it, then do it N more times" ([Kunz et al. 2025](https://icer2025.acm.org/details/icer-2025-papers/24/Unplugged-Scratch-and-Python-Tracing-Primary-Students-Preconceptions-of-Programmi)). Children with about a year of Logo modelled recursion as looping, even when the output contradicted them ([Kurland & Pea 1985](https://telearn.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00190537)).

| Area | What children get wrong | Evidence and ages |
|---|---|---|
| Superbug | The computer knows what I meant; names carry meaning | Pea 1986 (primary to college); Swidan 2018 (7 to 17) |
| Sequence | Order doesn't matter, or lines run at once | Swidan 2018, most common (7 to 17) |
| Variables | A box holds several values or remembers its history | Swidan 2018 (7 to 17); Grover & Basu 2017 (11 to 14) |
| Assignment | `=` read as maths equality | Swidan 2018, maths effect; Ma 2007 (university) |
| Loops | "Repeat 3" runs 4 times; loop bodies misread | Kunz 2025 (about 8 to 9); Grover & Basu 2017 |
| Booleans | AND and OR read as in English | Grover & Basu 2017; Franklin LT study, grade 4 ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10158761)) |
| Recursion | Recursion is just a loop | Kurland & Pea 1985 (Logo children) |
| References | Copying a name copies the thing | Ma 2007 (university only) |

Two lessons follow. First, much of the load at this age is **transfer from English and maths**: "repeat", "=", "and/or", meaningful names. A good course names the everyday, maths and computer meanings of each such word side by side. Second, **block languages prevent syntax errors but not misconceptions**. Grover and Basu's results come from children who had already finished a Scratch course, which is exactly the profile of the 11-year-old Jelly tester.

### Nine constructs and four thinking moves cover the minimum

Sequence, selection and iteration are theoretically sufficient for control flow (the Böhm–Jacopini result; the original paper was not retrieved this session, but the claim is textbook-standard). Every education framework adds named procedures, because they are needed for abstraction rather than computability. Brennan and Resnick's Scratch-derived framework lists seven concepts: sequences, loops, events, parallelism, conditionals, operators and data ([Brennan & Resnick 2012](https://scratched.gse.harvard.edu/ct/files/AERA2012.pdf)). The US K–12 framework treats abstraction as a *practice*, not a concept ([Maryland CS standards](https://www.marylandpublicschools.org/stateboard/Documents/01292018/ComputerScienceStandards.pdf)). Barefoot, England's primary framework, gives the most child-ready vocabulary: an algorithm is "a precise sequence of instructions, or set of rules, for performing a task", and abstraction is "identifying what is important and leaving out detail we do not need" ([Barefoot quick guide](https://www.barefootcomputing.org/docs/default-source/at-home/quick--guide-to-computational-thinking.pdf)).

The threshold-concept literature narrows the hard parts. "Abstraction" in general does not behave like a threshold, but particular concepts that depend on it do ([ECEASST summary](https://www.eceasst.org/index.php/eceasst/article/download/1983/2121)). The only school-focused work identifies **parameters, parameter passing and return values as a likely threshold conception**, and **procedural decomposition as a "threshold skill"** ([Kallia & Sentance 2021](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/229008)). Its earlier Delphi study with teachers agreed on 11 potential threshold concepts in functions alone ([Kallia & Sentance 2017](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/229017)). Threshold concepts are troublesome by definition, so a course should expect a period of mimicry and revisit functions in several contexts rather than "cover" them once.

For data, only variables and lists have evidence at this age. Hedy reaches lists as its only compound structure ([Hedy FOSDEM 2024 slides](https://archive.fosdem.org/2024/events/attachments/fosdem-2024-3550-hedy/slides/22768/Hedy_-_A_gradual_programming_language_YWEXvVn.pdf)). Records, lookup tables and trees are well motivated by computer science, but **no study of how 9-to-14-year-olds understand them was found**. Data representation (binary, text and images as numbers) is teachable unplugged. Binary was the strongest topic in one middle-school adaptation of CS Unplugged, but the main controlled study warns that unplugged activities which never say which computer idea they illustrate can *reduce* children's wish to study computing ([Taub, Armoni & Ben-Ari 2012](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci747s2c/lectures/taub-cs-unplugged-toce-12.pdf)).

### Transfer is real but near, so teach for it

The best meta-analysis of learning to program covers **105 studies and 539 effect sizes**. It finds overall transfer of **g = 0.49**, near transfer of **g = 0.75** and far transfer of **g = 0.47**. Gains appear in creativity, maths, metacognition, spatial skills and reasoning, while school achievement and literacy benefit least. Effects shrink against active control groups and in unpublished studies, which signals inflation ([Scherer, Siddiq & Sánchez Viveros 2019](https://nifu.brage.unit.no/nifu-xmlui/handle/11250/2639856)). Think Jelly can honestly promise that its ideas carry across programming languages and into the later Jelly courses. It should not promise better school grades.

## Reading before writing has the best evidence, but its effects are small

### PRIMM and predict-before-run are the backbone

PRIMM (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make) rests on five principles. Read before you write. Talk about programs. Focus on comprehension. Start from existing programs so that "the learner does not have responsibility for the code and does not suffer emotionally if the code has errors in". Take ownership gradually ([Sentance, RPF chapter](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2022/08/Teaching_Programming_with_PRIMM-1.pdf)). The one sizeable evaluation involved 13 schools in England, with **493 pupils aged 11 to 14 in the PRIMM group and 180 in the control group**, taught 10 Python lessons over 8 to 12 weeks. The PRIMM group did better on the post-test (**p = .001, r = .13**, a small effect). The design was quasi-experimental, the authors could not separate the stage structure from the materials, and the study was run by the method's designers with no independent replication ([Sentance, Waite & Kallia 2019](https://suesentance.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/teaching_computer_programming_with_primm__a_sociocultural_perspective_author_copy.pdf)). PRIMM's theory is sociocultural: classroom talk does much of the work. A self-guided browser page has to replace that talk some other way, and nothing has tested how.

The cleanest causal evidence for prediction comes from adults. **121 UCLA undergraduates with no coding experience** were randomly assigned either to predict output before being told how the code worked, or to be told and then practise. The predict group scored higher on learning (an effect of about 0.36) and on flexibility (about 0.47), and it felt more positive about coding. The authors' explanation fits children well: tell-then-practise makes novices meet error messages before they can read them ([Tucker et al. 2024](https://uclatall.com/pdfs/predictionversusproduction.pdf)). Behind both studies sits a strong university finding. Tracing and "explain in plain English" performance each correlate with code-writing performance ([Lopez et al. 2008](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/10806/1/2008001530.pdf)), and together they were later summarised as explaining **46% of the variance** in code writing ([Venables, Tan & Lister 2009](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/11384/1/2009002706OK.pdf)). In the 2004 multinational study, **23% of 556 students near the end of a first course scored 4 or fewer out of 12** on short tracing questions ([Lister et al. 2004](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)). England's national programme turns this into a rule: "focus first on code 'reading' activities, before code writing" ([Raspberry Pi pedagogy](https://www.raspberrypi.org/pedagogy)).

### Parsons problems, worked examples and fading remove load without removing thinking

For undergraduates, Parsons problems (dragging given lines into order) produced **the same learning gains as writing or fixing code in much less time**. Adaptive versions, which remove distractors or merge blocks when a learner struggles, helped more learners finish ([Ericson dissertation announcement](https://hg.gatech.edu/node/603172)). Offered as on-demand help during writing tasks, they improved practice efficiency with equal post-test scores ([Hou, Ericson & Wang 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10510493)). Child evidence is emerging: a study of 199 sixth graders exists, but its detailed results were not checked ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10644057)). One known risk is that learners put blocks in the right order without knowing why ([NSF PAR poster](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10510499)).

Worked examples with **subgoal labels** (short names for each step) showed medium to large effects across three pooled studies. Learners who wrote their own labels also did well ([Morrison, Margulieux & Decker 2020](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-curious-case-of-loops/)). In a full university course, subgoals did not raise exam means, but they produced **fewer drops and failures**, especially among at-risk students ([Margulieux, Morrison & Decker 2020](https://doaj.org/article/9a2c85d66fd54ec9ab019e9a510bbf17)). The results are not uniform; one 2015 replication did not find the expected gain ([RPF subgoal seminar](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)). The **expertise reversal effect** warns that guidance which helps novices can hurt learners who already know the material ([Reisslein et al. 2006](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)). That matters for a tester who arrives with Scratch and "JavaScript for Kids" already done.

### Productive failure works later and needs consolidation

Across 53 STEM studies, **problem solving followed by instruction beat instruction followed by problem solving by g = 0.36**, mainly on conceptual understanding and transfer. Two moderators matter here: **learners in grades 2 to 5 favoured instruction first**, and the struggle only paid off when explicit consolidation followed it ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105); [Kapur 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299996960_Examining_Productive_Failure_Productive_Success_Unproductive_Failure_and_Unproductive_Success_in_Learning)). The only programming study is tiny. With 16 analysed undergraduates, **direct instruction won immediately (8 of 9 against 2 of 7) and productive failure won two weeks later (7 of 7 against 6 of 9)**, with no inferential statistics ([arXiv 2411.11227](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11227v1)). The pattern is "worse now, better later". In a self-guided product with no teacher, failure that is not consolidated risks a child quitting.

### Gradual languages and the step from blocks to text are safe but unproven

Hedy is the clearest model of a language that grows. It starts with no brackets, colons or indentation and changes one rule at a time until it reaches a Python subset ([Hermans 2020](https://hedy.org/research/Hedy_A_Gradual_Language_for_Programming_Education_2020.pdf)). Its six design goals are directly reusable. Offer each concept at least three times. Start with the simplest form. Change only one aspect at a time. Defer punctuation as long as possible. Interleave new forms. Make every level able to produce meaningful programs. Evaluations are qualitative. **39 Dutch children aged 11 to 14, 80% of the experienced ones Scratch-only,** found Hedy easy and liked controlling the difficulty. They struggled with remembering commands and with quotes, and **disliked commands that changed form between levels** ([Gilsing & Hermans 2021](https://www.hedy.org/research/Gradual_Programming_in_Hedy_A_First_User_Study_2021.pdf); [Hedy 2022](https://hedy.org/research/Design_Implementation_and_evaluation_of_the_Hedy_programming_language_2022.pdf)). An undergraduate quasi-experiment with 87 students found **no performance difference** from using Hedy ([Karnalim et al. 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-53022-7_19)).

On blocks and text, the best evidence is from high school. Blocks gave larger early gains and more interest in further computing, while text felt more like "real" programming ([Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)). **After both groups moved to Java, the difference disappeared** ([Weintrop & Wilensky 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop&Wilensky_2019_C&E.pdf)). Bridges such as dual-view editors (Pencil Code's Droplet, MakeCode), frame-based editors and Blockly Games' Pond Tutor, which alternates block and typed-JavaScript levels, look promising but have small evidence, mostly "no worse than text" ([arXiv 2302.05708](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.05708); [Blockly Games](https://blockly.games/about)). For a child who has already done Scratch, starting back in blocks therefore buys little. Starting in text with strong scaffolds costs nothing measurable.

### Semantic waves, error messages and game mechanics shape the page

Semantic-wave theory says good explanations move from technical to concrete and **back up again**. It warns against "flatlines", for example a fun story never linked to the concept ([NCCE quick read](https://static.teachcomputing.org/pedagogy/QR6-Semantic-waves.pdf); [Waite et al. 2020](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/bitstream/123456789/66685/2/Waite%20Using%20Semantic%20Waves%202020%20Accepted.pdf)). Its evidence is case studies, with no effect sizes. Hedy found that children aged 11 to 14 **skip punctuation symbols when reading error messages aloud** ("You typed , but…" becomes "You typed but…"). Hedy now names characters in words ("comma") and offers "did you mean" fixes ([Hedy 2022](https://hedy.org/research/Design_Implementation_and_evaluation_of_the_Hedy_programming_language_2022.pdf)). Its logs show novices resubmitting identical faulty code without reading the error ([Hedy Gradual Feedback Model 2021](https://www.hedy.org/research/Introducing_the_Gradual_Feedback_Model_2021.pdf)). A programming-specific gamification meta-analysis found **d ≈ 0.4 driven by levels, while points, badges, avatars and leaderboards had no significant effect**; the studies were above school age ([ISCTE meta-analysis](https://ciencia.iscte-iul.pt/publications/using-game-concepts-to-improve-programming-learning-a-multi-level-meta-analysis/95533?lang=en)).

### Debugging must be taught as a procedure, because children default to guessing

Middle-school students "tend to edit code before locating the bugs, make frequent runs on minor edits, or treat the entire code as incorrect and start from scratch" ([Liu et al. 2017](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10074617)). Pupils in grades 4 and 5 who had been taught tracing still mostly debugged by swapping in simpler blocks ([SIGCSE 2023 poster](https://sigcse2023.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2023-posters/74/An-Exploration-of-Elementary-Students-Debugging-Behaviors-in-Scratch)). The NuzzleBug study with **125 pupils aged 12 to 16** taught a systematic debugging method *without* a tool first, then offered the debugger ([arXiv 2309.14465](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.14465)). No controlled trial of debugging instruction at 9 to 14 exists.

| Method | Best evidence | Population | Size of effect | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PRIMM | 13 schools, quasi-experimental | 11 to 14 | r = .13 (small) | Moderate; designers' own study |
| Predict before run | Randomised | Undergraduates | about 0.36 to 0.47 | Good, but adults |
| Parsons problems | Several studies | Undergraduates; one grade-6 study | Same gains, less time | Good for adults; child data unread |
| Subgoal labels | Pooled studies, one full course | Undergraduates | Medium to large; fewer failures | Mixed (one non-replication) |
| Productive failure | Meta-analysis, 53 studies | Mostly maths and science | g = 0.36; younger favour instruction first | Good outside programming |
| Gradual language (Hedy) | Surveys, logs | 11 to 14 | No learning-gain data | Weak |
| Blocks, then text | Quasi-experiments | High school | Early gain that washes out | Moderate |
| Semantic waves | Case studies | Teachers and pupils | None reported | Weak |
| Levels as gamification | Meta-analysis | Above school age | d about 0.4 | Moderate; not children |
| Explicit debugging | Small studies | 12 to 16 | Not measured | Weak |

### What these findings mean for a Jelly lesson

The following are recommendations drawn from the findings above, not findings themselves. The Jelly format (story, then "remember it", then mission) already traces one semantic wave. The story is the concrete dip. "Remember it" is where the idea gets packed into a technical word. The mission dips into concrete code again, and the end-of-mission card should climb back up by naming the idea and asking "where else would this work?". Inside each mission, follow PRIMM. Show a starter program the child does not own. Lock the Run button until the child commits a prediction. Show the prediction and the actual result side by side. Ask one investigate question ("which line made Wobble turn?"). Then ask for a small modification, and only then a make task. "Remember it" can be a worked example with three kid-language subgoal labels that the mission reuses as a checklist, fading over later lessons. Parsons ordering is the middle step between predicting and writing, followed by one "why does this line go here?" question. Error messages should name symbols in words, point at the line and offer a "did you mean". A typed "explain it in your own words" box, and an optional "explain it to a grown-up" prompt, stand in for PRIMM's classroom talk, though that substitution is untested. For the tester's profile, a "try it first" button lets experienced children skip the worked example, as expertise reversal predicts they should. For ages 11 to 14 only, some missions can open with a short productive-failure puzzle before the story, provided the story then explicitly compares the child's attempts with the canonical idea.

## Impossible, expensive and "at once" run ahead of every school curriculum

### No one has taught the halting problem to 9-to-14-year-olds

No peer-reviewed study teaching undecidability below high school was found. Formal treatment appears at 16 and over: in AP Computer Science Principles, which separates "undecidable" from "merely slow" ([Save My Exams AP CSP](https://www.savemyexams.com/ap/computer-science/college-board/principles/23/topic-questions/unit-3-algorithms-and-programming-/undecidable-problems/)), and in AQA A-level ([PMT AQA 4.4](https://pmt.physicsandmathstutor.com/download/Computer-Science/A-level/Notes/AQA/04-Theory-of-Computation/Advanced/4.4.%20Classification%20of%20Algorithms%20-%20Advanced.pdf)). The youngest classroom design found is an unplugged and Scratch activity for *high-school* students. It frames the halting problem as **the impossibility of a perfect malware detector** and uses a maze to introduce proof by contradiction; whether it was evaluated with students could not be retrieved ([Journault et al. 2020](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02559585)). The popular explainer is Pullum's Dr-Seuss-style verse proof "Scooping the Loop Snooper", published in *Mathematics Magazine* in 2000 ([York University copy](https://www.eecs.yorku.ca/~jeff/courses/fun/Halting_poetry.html)). It is hosted mainly on university course pages. The child-usable intuition is the asymmetry: **running a program can confirm that it stops, but no amount of waiting can confirm that it never will** ([Cornell CS172 handout](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs172/2003fa/handouts/L16.pdf)). University teachers also report that the proof is clearer told in terms of programs than Turing machines ([arXiv 1606.08703](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.08703)).

The same gap holds for testing. Dijkstra's "testing shows the presence, not the absence of bugs" dates from the 1969 NATO conference report ([Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Edsger_W._Dijkstra)), but **no school-level curriculum or study teaching the limits of testing was found**. School curricula ask for tracing and debugging, and the US middle-school standard asks pupils to "systematically test and refine programs using a range of test cases" ([CSTA 2-AP-17](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2022-12/cs-standards-6-8.pdf)). A memorable real-world anchor exists: England's legal presumption that computers work correctly helped the Post Office prosecute subpostmasters over Horizon shortfalls, until a 2024 Act quashed the remainder of **more than 700 wrongful convictions** ([Bentham's Gaze, UCL](https://www.benthamsgaze.org/2022/06/30/the-legal-rule-that-computers-are-presumed-to-be-operating-correctly-unforeseen-and-unjust-consequences/)).

### Cost intuition has ready-made unplugged material and a clear warning

Here the curriculum does reach the age group. England's KS3 (ages 11 to 14) asks pupils to "use logical reasoning to compare the utility of alternative algorithms for the same problem" ([gov.uk programmes of study](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-computing-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-computing-programmes-of-study)). New Zealand's Progress Outcome 4 has learners compare **linear and binary search by guessing a word's position in sorted and unsorted lists** ([Tāhūrangi PO4](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/computational-thinking---progress-outcome-4/5637168637.p)). CS Unplugged has a dedicated "Intractability" strand: graph colouring, dominating sets and Steiner trees, which are easy to state, easy to check and seemingly impossible to solve quickly. Its graph-colouring page connects them to the travelling salesperson problem, chess and Sudoku ([CS Unplugged graph colouring](https://classic.csunplugged.org/activities/graph-colouring/)). The evidence that unplugged work builds this intuition is thin. Its own founders note "relatively little systematic evaluation" ([Bell & Vahrenhold 2018](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/MJFCP7XA)). The best-known controlled study, with **12-to-13-year-olds in Israel, found that their ideas about computing partly improved but their desire to study it fell**. Only 11 of 24 activities made an explicit link to a computing concept *for students* ([Taub, Armoni & Ben-Ari 2012](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci747s2c/lectures/taub-cs-unplugged-toce-12.pdf)). Meta-analyses find both unplugged and plugged approaches help, and one finds plugged stronger (g = 0.818 against 0.649) ([Suparman et al. 2025](https://ejournal.uki.ac.id/index.php/edumatsains/article/view/6958)). The consensus is unplugged *bridged explicitly* to plugged. A browser sandbox can do both at once: a step counter makes cost visible on real runs.

### Concurrency is reachable at 10 but stays hard without targeted teaching

Concurrency is the one "limits" area with direct child evidence. A Scratch-based curriculum taught **Greek 10-year-olds with no programming background the basics of concurrency**, and 11-year-olds with some experience grasped more ([Fatourou et al. 2018](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jep/article/view/8216)). Israeli middle-schoolers in a two-year Scratch curriculum, however, kept struggling with concurrency, repeated execution and variables ([Meerbaum-Salant, Armoni & Ben-Ari 2013](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/learning-computer-science-concepts-with-scratch/)). The same authors worry that Scratch encourages "extremely fine-grained" bottom-up programming that may hinder later study ([Meerbaum-Salant et al. 2011](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/habits-of-programming-in-scratch/)). Ready-made activities exist for deadlock, the Orange Game, which CS Unplugged links to grades 3 to 5 ([CS Unplugged routing and deadlock](https://classic.csunplugged.org/activities/routing-and-deadlock/)), and for lost messages, the "Tablets of Stone" protocol game. In it, children discover that the internet "is not reliable and sometimes these messages get lost" and invent packet numbers ([CS Unplugged network protocols](https://classic.csunplugged.org/activities/network-protocols/)). The Australian version adds a natural cost lesson: every acknowledgement field takes space, so reliability needs more packets ([Australian Curriculum 5–6](https://www.Australiancurriculum.edu.au/media/5897/classroom-ideas-5-6-networks-unplugged.pdf)). **No child-level material was found for consensus, the Two Generals problem or eventual consistency**, and none for pseudo-randomness or floating-point surprises.

### How Think Jelly should teach the limits

What follows is recommendation. Think Jelly should sort every problem into "possible", "possible but too slow", or "impossible", and keep the third category as **intuition, not proof**, for the 9-to-11 track. The sandbox itself can supply the experiences. A step counter on every run makes cost visible: linear search in a 1,000-locker corridor against binary search in a sorted one. A doubling table shows why "try every input" testing cannot finish. A program taking two 32-bit numbers has 2⁶⁴ input pairs; this is an illustrative calculation, not a sourced activity. The interpreter's own **step limit** ("Wobble has taken 10,000 steps; is it stuck or just slow?") is the halting problem met in person. The mission then asks the child to design a perfect "stuck detector" and shows, through the malware-checker framing, a program that does the opposite of whatever the detector predicts. Pullum's poem suits ages 12 to 14 as a read-aloud. Concurrency should be *staged* rather than left to accident. Two robots share one counter and the child plays the scheduler, choosing whose step runs next until an update gets lost. Two robots each hold one key to a two-key door and deadlock. Messages between robots sometimes vanish. Each must end with an explicit bridge ("this is what servers face when two people press Save at once"), because Taub's study shows what happens when the link is left unsaid.

## Judgement is what survives when AI types the code

### Engineering habits are expected at 11 to 14 but rarely tested

US middle-school standards already expect pupils to test with a range of cases and to "document programs in order to make them easier to follow, test, and debug" ([CSTA standards review](https://csteachers.org/algorithms-and-programming-literature-review/)). Children test informally without being taught. **52 children aged 7 to 12 training and coding with machine-learning models "engage in the scientific method"**, including testing common cases ([Druga & Ko 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10281504)). The most teachable routine for turning that instinct into a habit is Bootstrap's Design Recipe. Write a contract (name, inputs, output). Write example inputs with expected outputs. Circle "what changes", which becomes the parameter ([Bootstrap Design Recipe](https://bootstrapworld.org/materials/fall2020/en-us/courses/algebra-pyret/lessons/alg-compound-inequalities/pages/onscreen.html)). Examples written first are tests, and they are also a specification, which is exactly what a person needs to hand an AI. Naming, simplicity and version-control habits have **no child-specific evidence**; they should be presented as professional conventions ("code is read much more often than it is written", in [PEP 8](https://peps.python.org/pep-0008/)'s words), not as proven learning results. Reading aloud improved syntax recall but not understanding in a study of 49 pupils aged 9 to 13 ([Swidan & Hermans 2019](https://serg.ewi.tudelft.nl/publications/the-effect-of-reading-code-aloud-on-comprehension-an-empirical-study-with-school-students/)).

### Children treat chatbots as friends while knowing they are programs

Children over-attribute intelligence to machines. Children aged 7 to 10 especially consider voice agents "more intelligent than themselves" ([Druga & Ko 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10281504)). A UK survey of **1,000 children aged 9 to 17** found 64% use AI chatbots. **40% of users had no concerns about following chatbot advice** and 36% were unsure, and 35% said it feels like talking to a friend ([Internet Matters 2025](https://www.internetmatters.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Me-Myself-AI-Report.pdf)). A preprint review of 35 studies describes "dual consciousness": children hold technical knowledge and social attribution at the same time, as a suspension of disbelief rather than confusion. It argues that "the conversational layer is the anthropomorphic layer", so simply telling children "it's not a person" is likely to fail ([Jayathilake & Ma 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18250v1)). The evidence for what works points to **building and breaking**. After four weeks of training models, children "became more skeptical" and shifted agency "from the devices to the people who program them" ([Druga & Ko 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10281504)). **48 middle-schoolers aged 10 to 14** who built chatbots with hallucination-awareness scaffolds improved their AI knowledge and their awareness of hallucination; the study was pre/post with no control group, and only its abstract was read ([CHI hallucination study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791480)). LLMs Unplugged has a Year 5 class (about age 10) build a word-follows-word model by hand from a children's book and generate text with dice. The aim is to show that ChatGPT "works the same way" ([ANU LLMs Unplugged](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.13183)). The Raspberry Pi Foundation's Experience AI unit for 11-to-14-year-olds teaches that "LLMs are prediction machines" ([RPF Experience AI](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/experience-ai-new-updated-lessons/)). RPF also advises system verbs ("detect", "generate", "pattern match") instead of "AI learns" or "AI sees"; this is reasoned argument, not an experiment ([RPF anthropomorphism blog](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/ai-education-anthropomorphism/)).

### What still matters when AI writes code

The debate the name "Think Jelly" sidesteps is real. A 2025 Dagstuhl seminar said generative AI has "likely rendered" the traditional introductory programming approach obsolete ([Dagstuhl 25311](https://www.dagstuhl.de/25311)), and some educators argue for "prompts first, precision later" ([arXiv 2407.09231](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.09231)). The strongest steelman is that natural language is simply the next layer of abstraction. Even that view leaves the thinking intact, though, because someone still has to know what to ask for and whether the answer is right. The empirical picture supports this. In the one study with children, **69 novices aged 10 to 17** using an AI code generator completed **1.15 times as many tasks with 1.8 times the scores** on writing tasks. They did no worse on later manual modification tasks, and those with stronger prior Scratch scores benefited most ([Kazemitabaar et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.07427)). Among university novices, students who already had a plan used AI to accelerate and ignored wrong suggestions, while struggling students were hindered: **"the widening gap"** ([Prather et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17739v1)). The Raspberry Pi Foundation's position paper distinguishes coding from programming, which is "formulating a problem in a way that it can be solved by a computer". It notes that LLMs "generate statistically acceptable outputs" with "no guarantee of accuracy", and argues that "the friction introduced in the conversion of human reasoning into a rigid expression of logic is where the learning… occurs" ([RPF position paper 2025](https://static.raspberrypi.org/files/about/Why-kids-still-need-to-learn-to-code-in-the-age-of-AI-2025-Raspberry-Pi-Foundation-position-paper.pdf)). That is a position, not new evidence. RPF's 2026 unit compresses the advice into "think first, prompt second" ([RPF blog](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/encouraging-learners-to-think-first-prompt-second-using-large-language-models-to-learn/)).

Synthesising these, the skills that survive under every view are understanding and decomposing the problem, specifying it precisely with examples, reading and predicting code, testing (while knowing tests cannot prove correctness), debugging from evidence, judging cost and limits, and owning the consequences. Typing syntax is the part most exposed to automation, and it is the part Think Jelly deliberately spends least time on. This is the honest answer for parents who ask why the course exists: **AI can type code; an engineer decides what to build, says exactly what it should do, checks that it does it, and is responsible for what happens next.**

### Ethics and myth-busting work best woven through, using real cases

Every major AI-literacy framework embeds ethics: UNESCO's "ethics of AI" dimension ([UNESCO framework](https://raise.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/391105eng.pdf)), AI4K12's central "Societal Impact" idea ([AI4K12](https://ai4k12.org/gradeband-progression-charts/)), and MIT's Day of AI, which weaves "ethical, social, and policy considerations throughout technical explanations" ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/policy-politics/day-of-ai-spurs-classroom-discussions-on-societal-impacts-of-artificial-intelligence/2023/05)). No evaluation data for ethics lessons at this age was found. Two real cases do much of the work. The PULSE "depixelation" tool turned a pixelated photo of Barack Obama into a white man's face. It did not recover detail; it searched generated faces for a plausible match, and its training data was reported as about 90% white ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/06/24/ai_image_tool/)). That one example busts the film "zoom and enhance" myth, shows hallucination, and shows bias inherited from data. Horizon busts "computers don't make mistakes". The Hollywood hacking myths (frantic typing, two people on one keyboard, passwords cracked in seconds, instant perfect database hits) are well documented in security press ([CSO Online](https://www.csoonline.com/article/557699/hollywoods-5-biggest-hacking-myths.html)), but **no curriculum-grade myth-busting lesson and no data on how many children hold these myths were found**.

## Think Jelly in 22 missions, from obedient robot to front desk

Everything in this section is recommendation. It builds on the findings above, on the Jelly series' constraints, and on the earlier Web part 2 research. That research found that every national progression checked puts **a text programming language directly after web development**: England's Teach Computing goes from "Developing for the Web" to "Introduction to Python programming", and Code.org goes from Web Development to JavaScript games ([Teach Computing KS3](https://teachcomputing.org/curriculum/key-stage-3); [Code.org CSD](https://code.org/files/CSDiscoveries_1-pager.pdf)). The agreed chain after Web Jelly is now Server, then Storage, then SQL, so Think Jelly sits between Security and Server and must hand Server a learner who can write a function that takes a request and returns a response.

### Design rules for the pretend robot and its growing language

**One robot, honest about being dumb.** Call it Wobble, a jelly robot on a grid. It does exactly what it is told, in order, and nothing else, which makes it the anti-superbug. Contrast it explicitly with Robo, Security Jelly's AI helper, which *guesses*. That contrast becomes the backbone of the AI arc: Wobble follows instructions, Robo predicts text.

**Real JavaScript from line one, as a strict subset that only grows.** Hedy's most-cited pain point was syntax that changed between levels ([Gilsing & Hermans 2021](https://www.hedy.org/research/Gradual_Programming_in_Hedy_A_First_User_Study_2021.pdf)), and the earlier tools research advised against any pseudo-language that later has to be unlearned. So every program a child writes should be valid JavaScript, and every level should be a superset of the last. Tell children on day one that Wobble understands "a small piece of JavaScript, the language web pages speak, and each lesson unlocks more", which also respects the tester's "JavaScript for Kids" background. Commands are function calls (`move()`, `turnLeft()`), and lesson 1 teaches the brackets as "do it now". Leave out semicolons, and have the level checker forbid lines that begin with `(` or `[`, the main case where JavaScript's automatic semicolon insertion misbehaves. Teach `===` only, and teach `=` as "put into", drawn as an arrow into a box. Avoid the classic `for (let i = 0; i < 4; i++)` loop. Instead, provide a given word `times(4)` that returns `[0, 1, 2, 3]`, so the first loop is `for (const step of times(4)) { move() }`. That exact form later loops over lists (`for (const pet of pets)`), so the form never changes, only what goes after `of`. This is a design bet: it keeps Hedy's one-change-at-a-time rule and matches modern server code, at the cost of postponing the C-style loop to Server Jelly or later.

**Enforce "taught before used" mechanically.** Parse with Acorn and check each level against a whitelist of syntax node types and identifiers, giving a Hedy-style message for anything not yet taught ("Wobble doesn't know `while` yet; it arrives in mission 8"). This is the same concept-registry idea the series already uses for shell commands. For execution, the earlier tools research found Neil Fraser's JS-Interpreter proven, sandboxed and steppable one operation at a time. However, it is **ES5 only and about 200 times slower than native**, so `let`, `const` and arrow functions would need transpiling ([JS-Interpreter docs](https://neil.fraser.name/software/JS-Interpreter/docs.html)). Sval supports modern syntax but has no documented stepping ([Sval](https://github.com/Siubaak/sval)). Because the Think Jelly subset is small, a small custom tree-walking stepper over the Acorn syntax tree is probably simplest. It would give line highlighting, a variables panel, a call-stack panel, a step counter, a step limit, and a scheduler the child can drive for the two-robot missions. JS-Interpreter, with a Babel step, is the fallback. These engineering judgements have not yet been tested in a build.

**Name the clashes with earlier Jelly courses.** Children arrive with shell meanings that collide with JavaScript. In the Jelly shell, `&&` and `||` mean "only if the last command worked" and "only if it failed", decided by exit codes. In JavaScript they combine true and false. Worse, **a shell exit code of 0 means success, while in JavaScript 0 counts as false**. `*` already means "any filename" in the shell and will mean "multiply" here. Each clash should get a "same symbol, different world" card the first time it appears, the same treatment the earlier research recommended for `*` in SQL.

**Every mission keeps the PRIMM shape and the evidence-light extras stay optional.** The mission sequence is: predict (Run locked), run, compare side by side, one investigate question, modify, then make. Levels and unlocks are the reward; points and leaderboards are not, because they have no supporting evidence. Every mission ends by naming the idea and asking where else it applies. An "explain it in your own words" box replaces classroom talk.

Four ordering choices deserve explanation, because the existing courses disagree. **Named procedures come before loops (mission 4 before 5)**, as in Stanford's Karel ([Karel reader](https://compedu.stanford.edu/karel-reader/docs/python/en/intro.html)), because "turn right is three left turns" is the canonical first abstraction in a robot world. Code.org, by contrast, waits until Course F for variables ([Code.org CSF](https://code.org/csf)). **Variables come after loops but before conditions**, because Swidan found older children handle variables better, and because sensing conditions make more sense once there is state to test. **Parameters and return values sit at the end of Arc 3, after variables**, and are revisited in missions 16, 20 and 22, because they are the threshold concept and need several passes. **Limits come after data**, because cost needs lists to search and tests need functions to test. Hedy and BJC Sparks show that lists can come much earlier when they unlock fun programs ([Hedy 2020](https://hedy.org/research/Hedy_A_Gradual_Language_for_Programming_Education_2020.pdf); [BJC Sparks](https://bjc.berkeley.edu/sparks)). If pilots show children bored by Arc 3, pulling a simple list forward is the first change to try. No study compares concept orderings at this age, so any of these choices can be revised from pilot data.

### What Server Jelly inherits

Mission 22 is deliberately the shape of a server handler without any HTTP. The child registers a function, the world calls it with a record describing who knocked, and the function returns a record describing the reply. The earlier Web part 2 research recommended exactly this shape for the pretend server: a JavaScript routing table such as `"GET /pets" → (request) => html`, with every exchange shown as a pair of "letters" to and from the server so children do not conclude "it all happened in my browser" ([CS Teaching Tips](https://www.csteachingtips.org/tip/misconception-students-may-not-realize-websites-need-and-use-servers-store-process-and-deliver)). Server Jelly can therefore assume functions with parameters and return values, records and dot notation, lists of records, `for … of`, `if`/`else` with `===`, strings and the `+` join, examples written as tests, the first-surprise debugging method, and the idea that a function can be handed to the world to call later. Its own first lessons then only need to add the HTTP-specific parts the child already knows from Web Jelly: methods, paths, query strings and status codes, now arriving as fields on a request record. Server should teach building HTML from strings *and escaping it* there, where the Security course's XSS lesson pays off. It should also teach the idea of "waiting for something slow" (asynchronous code) there, at the point of need.

Three Think Jelly experiences are planted specifically to motivate the courses after Server. Mission 6's boxes vanish when the page reloads, which is the reason Storage exists. Mission 18's lost update is what happens when two requests change one counter, the reason databases have transactions. Mission 12's list of cards, seen as a table, is the first glimpse of SQL. Mission 14's step counter returns in SQL as the index lesson, where the earlier research measured a search stepping through 99,999 rows without an index and none with one.

### What to pilot before building everything

The evidence leaves four questions that only learners can answer. The first is whether 9-to-10-year-olds can handle parameters and return values in mission 9, or whether the core track should stop at named procedures and leave parameters to a second pass. The second is whether the `times(4)` loop form reads naturally to children or confuses them more than Hedy's `repeat 4 times`. The third is whether a written "explain it" box recovers any of PRIMM's talk benefit at home. The fourth is whether the "stuck detector" mission builds the intended intuition at 9 to 11 or only at 12 to 14. A pilot of missions 1 to 9 plus 14, 17 and 22 with mixed ages, with prediction accuracy logged on every mission, would answer most of these cheaply. That accuracy log is also the best built-in assessment the course can have, since tracing and explaining predict writing.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what a "thinking" course is for. Its job is not to postpone code but to make the computer's behaviour *predictable* to a child: a small machine whose every step can be guessed before it happens, checked after, and explained in plain words. Prediction is the one skill that runs through every layer of the minimum. It is how children learn constructs, how they debug, how they meet the limits (a prediction that never resolves is the halting problem; a prediction that takes forever is cost; a prediction that depends on who goes first is a race), and how they judge AI output. A course organised around "predict, then check" is therefore the same course whether or not machines write most code in future. That is the strongest justification for the name Think Jelly.

The evidence's main weakness is also a design opportunity. Almost every strong finding comes from university students or classroom teachers, and nothing at all has been measured for self-guided 9-to-14-year-olds in a browser. Think Jelly can log every prediction a child makes and whether it was right. With that log, the series would become a source of the child-level evidence the field lacks, not just a consumer of adult evidence. The limits arc and the AI arc have no curricular precedent at this age, so they are where Think Jelly is most original and where it most needs that measurement.
